# Part I: Foundations

## Understanding the Lifts — Phases

All three lifts (snatch, clean and jerk) can be understood in two fundamental phases:

1. **Upward phase:** the lifter elevates the barbell with the lower body
2. **Downward phase:** the lifter moves their body down underneath the elevated barbell with the upper body

**Three-pull system (used throughout this book):**

| Phase | Description |
|---|---|
| **Preparatory Position** | Relaxed or semi-relaxed position at the bar before actively setting the start |
| **Starting Position** | The last position before any elevation of the barbell from the floor |
| **First Pull** | Bar from floor to approximately mid-thigh, where the final explosion is initiated |
| **Second Pull** | The final upward explosion: from mid-thigh through full extension of knees and hips (includes the double knee bend / transition / scoop) |
| **Third Pull** | Active relocation of the lifter under the barbell — the descent |
| **Receiving Position** | Overhead in the snatch; on the shoulders in the clean |
| **Recovery** | Movement from the receiving position to standing |

**Russian six-phase system (three periods):**

*Period 1 — The Pull:*
- Phase 1: Force application beginning to bar separation from floor
- Phase 2: From bar separation to beginning of double knee bend

*Period 2 — The Explosion:*
- Phase 3: Double knee bend / scoop (first part of second pull)
- Phase 4: Final upward extension from greatest knee flexion to complete body extension

*Period 3 — The Squat Under:*
- Phase 5: Lifter's initial movement under the bar to barbell's maximum height
- Phase 6: From barbell's maximum height to receiving position

**Jerk phases:**

| Phase | Description |
|---|---|
| **Starting Position** | Last position before initiating the dip |
| **Dip** | Bending of the knees to elastically load muscles and the barbell |
| **Drive** | Aggressive push against the ground to accelerate and elevate the barbell maximally |
| **Push Under** | Active push against the bar with the arms (analogous to the third pull) |
| **Receiving Position** | Typically the split position; may also be power jerk squat |
| **Recovery** | Standing from split or squat to final standing position |

## Learning & Teaching the Lifts

**Core principle:** There is a remarkably narrow band of truly effective technique for most athletes — individual variation exists but is minor compared to the fundamental commonalities among elite lifters worldwide.

**The teaching progression:** Teach a complete movement pattern from the simplest version toward the full lift. The learning progression used in this book works from the receiving position backward to the full lift — establishing the end-point first ensures the foundation is correct before building the movement.

**For beginning lifters:**
- Use a PVC pipe (5-foot, ¾") or a technique barbell (5–10 kg) — inexpensive, allows any athlete to manage large volumes of drills, and allows drills that are impossible with a regulation bar
- Do not introduce loading beyond the technique bar until the positions and movements are consistently correct
- Focus on movement quality, not weight

**Communication and cueing:**
- Keep technique instructions practical and concise during training — theory and extensive rationale should be reserved for discussions outside training sessions
- An instruction overloaded with information spreads the athlete's focus too thin
- Every athlete responds to coaching differently; the coach must adapt approaches to the individual

**For coaches:**
- The quality of a coach is defined not only by the ability to diagnose errors but to simplify corrections for the athlete
- Understanding lift mechanics is the only way to devise corrective strategies for any conceivable error independently rather than relying on memorized prescriptions
- Athletes training without a coach are their own coaches and should read all coach-directed sections accordingly

## Individual Variation

Athletes vary in nearly every physically relevant dimension: limb proportions, torso length, hip structure, shoulder mobility, ankle mobility, and motor learning ability. These variations dictate that some modification of standard technique is necessary or inevitable for each athlete.

**Key principle:** The goal is not to force every athlete into an identical position, but to apply the underlying biomechanical principles that produce effective and safe lifting. How those principles are expressed will look different on different athletes.

**Proportional extremes and implications:**
- **Long femurs, short torsos:** The athlete will have a more inclined torso in the starting position and first pull. This is not a fault — it is a biomechanical necessity.
- **Narrow hips / limited external rotation:** May require a narrower stance or different foot angle.
- **Limited shoulder mobility:** May require a wider overhead squat stance or limit the viable snatch grip width.
- **Long arms:** May lower the contact point of the bar on the body in the second pull, requiring an adjusted grip width.

**Training response:**
- Athletes vary significantly in how they respond to training stimuli, how quickly they recover, and the optimal volume/intensity ratio for their progress
- A program that works well for one athlete may be ineffective or counterproductive for another even with apparently similar athletes
- This variability requires coaches to observe, evaluate, and adapt programming regularly

## Facility & Equipment

**Platform:** A 4m × 4m (minimum) lifting platform is ideal. Platforms protect the floor and the barbell. Wood is the traditional and best material.

**Barbell:** Competition-quality bars are essential — a men's bar weighs 20 kg, women's bar weighs 15 kg. The bar must have bearings (not bushings) to allow the knurled sleeve to spin independently of the shaft during the lift.

**Bumper plates:** Necessary for safe dropping of the barbell from overhead. Do not train on iron-only plates.

**Collars:** Always use collars. Unsecured plates shift during a lift and can cause imbalance or injury.

**Pulling blocks:** Useful for work from specific heights without dropping the bar to the floor. Come in multiple heights.

**Jerk boxes / jerk blocks:** Raised supports at shoulder height used for jerk practice without requiring the athlete to clean the bar first.

**Shoes:** Weightlifting shoes are mandatory. The elevated heel (typically 22–25 mm) allows the proper squat depth and upright torso angle without requiring extraordinary ankle mobility. A flat shoe is inappropriate for Olympic weightlifting.

**Belt:** Use is optional and situational. A belt provides external support for the trunk when used appropriately during heavy pulling and squatting. It does not replace the necessity of proper breathing and bracing.

**Knee sleeves:** Provide warmth, support, and proprioceptive feedback for the knees. Appropriate for training and competition.

**Wrist wraps:** Support the wrists in the overhead position and during pulls. Not a substitute for developing wrist strength and mobility.

**Straps:** Useful for pulls and pulls-based exercises where grip fatigue would limit training quality. Should not be used for the snatch and clean themselves — grip strength for the competition lifts must be developed.

**Chalk:** Essential for the hook grip. Apply sparingly — excess chalk reduces friction.

## Warming Up

The warm-up serves multiple purposes: raising core temperature, increasing blood flow to muscles, improving joint mobility, activating neurological drive, and preparing the athlete psychologically for training.

**Structure:**
1. **Soft-tissue work / self-myofascial release (SMR):** foam rolling on areas specific to the athlete's needs
2. **Dynamic range of motion (DROM) exercises:** active, controlled movement through full ranges — not static stretching
3. **Technique primer exercises:** light snatch/clean/jerk derivatives to prepare the specific movement patterns

**Key points:**
- The warm-up is not just "light versions of training" — it has specific purposes
- The body needs to be physiologically warm before static stretching is appropriate; never static stretch cold
- For heavy training days, the warm-up should be thorough enough that no warm-up sets are needed in the technical sense — the athlete is fully prepared before approaching working weights

**Activation and preparation work:**
- Hip flexor and thoracic spine mobility are almost universally important for weightlifters
- Shoulder mobility and stability exercises should be included regularly
- The athlete should finish the warm-up sweating lightly and moving freely

## Breathing & Trunk Rigidity

**Fundamental principle:** The trunk must be rigid to transfer force from the legs to the barbell during the pull, and to support the barbell safely in the overhead and rack positions. This rigidity is achieved through pressurization of the abdominal and thoracic cavities, not by muscular strength alone.

**Why pressurization matters:**
- Intraabdominal pressure can reduce pressure on intervertebral discs by 20% on average and as much as 40% (Zatsiorsky 1995)
- The torso's weakest compressible region is below the ribcage — the area between the ribcage and pelvis lacking rigid skeletal structure on the sides and front
- Filling the lungs increases rigidity of the thoracic cavity and forces the diaphragm down, compressing the abdominal organs
- Tightening the circumferential musculature of the trunk prevents the container walls from expanding and reduces torso collapse potential

**Muscular force and breath:**
- Muscular force is greater with the Valsalva maneuver (breath held, trunk pressurized) than with exhalation or normal breathing
- Holding the breath during a lifting effort increases muscles' ability to generate force (Zatsiorsky 1995)

**Execution:**
1. Inhale deeply — allow the abdomen to expand outward (diaphragmatic breathing), not just chest expansion. Initiate through the nose if possible.
2. Hold the breath throughout the most demanding portion of the lift (typically the entire first and second pull, or the entire jerk drive)
3. Exhale forcefully after the most critical demand has passed (e.g., during the recovery of a squat, or after securing the jerk overhead)
4. Never exhale on the way up during the pull — this reduces trunk rigidity at the worst possible moment

**Common error:** Drawing the abdomen in (sucking in the belly) — this is the opposite of what is needed. The abdomen should expand outward to allow the diaphragm to fully contract and fill the lungs.

**Belt use:** A belt provides an external surface to push against with the abdominal wall, enhancing the effect of the pressurization. However, the breathing and bracing mechanics must be correct regardless of belt use.

## The Squat

The squat is foundational to weightlifting — both the overhead squat (snatch receiving position) and front squat (clean receiving position) are essential positions.

**General squat mechanics:**
- Feet approximately shoulder width apart, toes turned out at a natural angle (typically 30° or less)
- Knee tracking over the toes — knees should travel in the direction of the toes, not collapse inward
- Bar over midfoot throughout the movement
- Descent is controlled and active; the lifter should not simply fall into the bottom position
- The bottom position (full squat) requires the hip crease below the top of the knee

**Foot stance for weightlifting:**
- The squat stance is typically somewhat wider than the pulling (first and second pull) stance
- The transition from pulling stance to squat stance during the third pull is a critical technical element

**Front squat specifics:**
- Bar rests on the "rack" — the front deltoids and clavicles — with elbows high
- The torso must remain as upright as possible to prevent the bar from rolling forward
- Grip on the bar can be full (all fingers under) or fingertip (a few fingers under) depending on shoulder mobility
- Elbows must remain high throughout to maintain the rack position

**Overhead squat specifics:**
- Bar is balanced directly over the shoulder blades / base of neck
- Shoulder blades actively retracted and upwardly rotated
- Arms pushed up against the bar aggressively to maintain an active lockout
- The torso angle, like the front squat, should be as upright as possible
- The bottom position is especially demanding of mobility — hip, thoracic, and shoulder

**Squat depth:** Full depth (hip crease below the top of the knee) is required in competition and should be trained consistently. Partial squats for training purposes should be clearly labeled as such.

**Spinal position:** Maintain the natural lumbar curve — neither excessively arched (hyperextended) nor rounded (flexed). Neutral spine maximizes structural integrity and force transfer.

---

## Foot Positions & Transition

**Two distinct foot positions exist in the snatch and clean:**

1. **Pull stance (first and second pull):** Hip-width or slightly narrower. This narrower stance allows the bar to travel close to the body and maximizes vertical force production in the explosion.

2. **Squat stance (receiving position):** Typically wider than pull stance. The wider stance accommodates the depth of the squat and the placement of the elbows (clean) or arms (snatch).

**The transition** between pull stance and squat stance occurs during the third pull — the athlete slides the feet outward as they descend under the bar. This transition must be:
- Quick — the feet must reach the squat stance before or as the athlete reaches the receiving position
- Precise — both feet should land in a position that allows immediate squat support
- Consistent — the athlete must land in the same stance every repetition

**Common errors:**
- Feet landing too narrow — forces a partial squat or prevents full depth
- Feet landing too wide — reduces structural stability, strains the hips and knees
- One foot landing before the other — creates lateral imbalance
- Jumping backward during the transition — moves the bar forward relative to the body

**Training the transition:**
- Many of the learning drills (tall snatch, tall clean) emphasize precisely this transition
- The transition should be practiced as part of the full lift as well as in isolation

## The Hook Grip

The hook grip is a pronated (palms-down) grip in which the thumb is trapped between the bar and the first one or two fingers.

**Why the hook grip is mandatory:**
- During the violent explosion of the second pull, a conventional overhand grip is insufficient to maintain bar control — the bar will spin out of the hand
- The hook grip creates a system of countering hooks: the bar tends to roll out of the thumb in one direction and out of the fingers in the opposite, eliminating the spinning element of grip loss
- The increased security of the hook grip allows lower overall grip tension, which reduces elbow tension and improves the speed and fluidity of the second-to-third pull transition
- Activation of finger and wrist flexors (from gripping tightly) inhibits activation of elbow extensors — lower grip tension means better third-pull speed

**How to apply the hook grip:**
1. Wrap the thumb fully around the bar (not parallel to it)
2. Wrap the index and middle fingers over the thumb and around the bar
3. The thumb reaches around the bar enough to contribute significantly to grip integrity, and the fingers hook onto the thumb — creating a strong ridge

**Pain and adaptation:**
- The hook grip is uncomfortable and even painful for the first few weeks of use — this is universal and temporary
- The thumb and fingers will adapt through callus formation and structural adaptation within a few weeks
- Tape can be applied over the thumb to reduce discomfort during the adaptation period

**Hook grip in the overhead position:**
- Whether to maintain or release the hook grip at the overhead position is individual
- Athletes who can achieve the correct hand/wrist position and elbow extension speed with the hook grip should maintain it
- Athletes who cannot (typically men with thick hands and larger bars) should release it as the bar passes overhead
- The decision must be made per athlete through experimentation

## The Double Knee Bend

The double knee bend (also called the scoop or transition) is a phenomenon — not an intentional action — that occurs at the initiation of the second pull.

**What happens:**
- As the bar reaches approximately mid-thigh and the knees have achieved partial extension during the first pull, the final violent hip extension begins
- As hip extension begins, the knees shift forward slightly and cease their extension momentarily (or very slightly re-bend)
- The knees then finish their extension along with the remaining hip extension as the athlete reaches the power position

**Why it occurs:**
1. The hamstrings are biarticular — they cross both the hip and knee joints. Contraction to extend the hip simultaneously creates a pull to flex the knee, preventing the knees from finishing extension and maintaining the partially-bent angle.
2. As the trunk moves up and back from hip extension, the hips and knees must shift forward to maintain balance of the bar-body system.

**The power position:**
The maximal forward position of the knees, at the point where the torso reaches approximately vertical, hips under shoulders, and bar at or just above the crease of the hips. This is the position from which the final explosion is delivered.

**Key point:** The double knee bend is unavoidable with correct lifting mechanics — it is not a technique the athlete must intentionally perform. Attempts to consciously "do the double knee bend" are counterproductive. The focus should be on initiating the final explosion with the bar at the right height and maintaining continuous aggressive leg drive.

**Degree of flexion:** Ideally minimal. Shorter-legged, leg-dominant lifters will typically show a more pronounced scoop; tall, hip-dominant lifters less so.

## Starting Position Principles

The starting position applies equally to both the snatch and the clean (with only grip width and foot placement differing). Its primary purpose is to create the optimal position, balance, and positional relationship of the barbell and body for the first and second pulls.

**The fundamental requirement:** Set the lifter up for ideal mechanics in the second pull — the heart of the lift, in which the barbell receives its critical upward acceleration.

**Criteria for the starting position:**

1. **Bar over the midfoot:** The center of mass of the bar-body system must be over the base of support throughout the lift. Starting with the bar over the midfoot (approximately 1 inch from the shins) ensures this.

2. **Upright posture (not excessive forward lean):**
   - Minimizes hip and lumbar torque and fatigue of spinal extensors during the first pull
   - Allows greater hip extension speed during the second pull
   - Shorter rotational distance during the second pull
   - Encourages the bar to remain close to the body
   - Athletes feel more comfortable and consequently perform better psychologically

3. **Specific joint angles:**
   - **Hips:** Above the knees in height, but below shoulder height
   - **Back angle:** Approximately 30–40° forward of vertical in most athletes (varies with proportions)
   - **Shoulders:** Slightly in front of or directly over the bar
   - **Knees:** Pushed out over the toes, aligned with the direction of the feet

4. **Foot width:** Narrower than the squat stance — hip-width or slightly narrower, toes turned out at the athlete's natural angle

5. **Arms:** Straight, vertical or slightly in front of vertical; elbows rotated out (internally rotated upper arm)

6. **Head and eyes:** Looking forward or slightly upward — maintaining this throughout the first pull

7. **Trunk rigidity:** Full breath taken and held, trunk fully pressurized before any movement

**Dynamic vs. static start:**
- **Static:** Lifter lowers into the position and initiates from a fully set, stationary position. More consistent and repeatable — the lifter stores energy in the stretched hamstrings similar to a compressed spring. Used by Naim Suleymanoglu among others.
- **Dynamic:** Lifter uses a hip dip motion — a quick lowering of the hips before initiating — to create an impulse of energy. More complex and easily mistimed. Used by Nikolai Peschalov.
- Both are valid; the static start is generally recommended for most athletes due to repeatability.

**Separation of the bar from the floor:**
- Should be smooth and controlled — not jerked or exploded
- The bar should be lifted with the legs, maintaining the back angle, not by immediately extending the hips
- Any sudden acceleration at liftoff disrupts balance and bar path

# Part II: The Snatch

## Grip Placement

**Principle:** The optimal snatch grip width is that which places the bar in the crease of the hips in an extended body position — the ideal contact point for the final explosion.

**How to find the grip width:**
1. Stand upright and hold the bar with a hook grip at arms' length
2. Adjust hand position until the bar rests in the hip crease (just above the pubic bone)
3. This position accounts for arm length, shoulder width, and their relationship to torso and leg proportions

**Grip width spectrum — tradeoffs:**

| Direction | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Wider grip** | Shorter bar travel distance; less shoulder mobility demand overhead; keeps CoM lower; better bar proximity during third pull; faster turnover | More difficult starting position; greater wrist/hand strain overhead; more difficult bar grip during pull; reduced structural integrity overhead; more misses behind |
| **Narrower grip** | Stronger, more comfortable start/first pull; better grip integrity; improved shoulder structure overhead | Longer bar travel; more shoulder mobility required; harder to keep bar close in third pull; speed/balance/accuracy problems in second pull |

**Adjustments:** The grip should be noted relative to knurling markings on the bar for consistent placement. Small adjustments can be made based on individual strengths and weaknesses as the athlete progresses.

---

## The Overhead Position

**The overhead structure** must be maximally rigid — any reduction in effort will result in sudden, unpredictable bar movement.

**Shoulder blade position:**
- Forceful retraction and upward rotation of the scapulae — the foundation of the overhead position
- This anchors the arms securely to the body and prevents injurious shifts in shoulder position
- **Not scapular elevation (shrugging)** — this reduces stability

**To achieve correct overhead position:**
1. Place the bar across the shoulders behind the neck (back squat position)
2. Squeeze upper inside edges of shoulder blades together
3. Press the bar straight up — maintaining torso inclination and shoulder blade position
4. The bar should finish directly above the base of the neck

**Elbow orientation:** Bony point of elbows directed approximately halfway between backward and down (mid-point between full internal and full external rotation). This provides maximum structural stability:
- Opens the shoulder joint for full arm elevation
- Angles the elbow articulation away from the downward force line, allowing the skeleton to assist in support

**Head position:** Must push forward through the arms — attempts to maintain a completely vertical head will prevent adequate scapular retraction

**Arm position:** Arms oriented approximately vertical when viewed from the side; bar placed over the top of the shoulder blades / base of neck

**Elbow lockout:** Absolutely required for competition legality and structural stability. Even slight elbow flexion dramatically reduces supportable load.

**Hand and wrist position:**
- Bar in the palm, slightly behind the center of the forearm
- Wrist allowed to extend back; heel of palm driven up aggressively
- Grip as relaxed as possible while maintaining control (not tight-gripping)
- A tight grip overhead inhibits elbow extension speed and prevents proper bar settling into the palm
- The correct position creates a slight tendency for the bar to move backward — this makes stabilization simple. A neutral wrist position (which causes the bar to shift both forward and backward equally) creates instability.

## Learning the Snatch — Progression

The learning progression works backward from the receiving position to the full lift:

### Step 1: Receiving Position Work
1. **Grip placement** — establish correct grip width
2. **Overhead position** — establish correct scapular and arm position
3. **Overhead squat** — full overhead squat with the snatch grip; emphasize active arm position throughout

### Step 2: Overhead Squat Variations
4. **Pressing snatch balance** — from behind the neck, press the bar to overhead while descending into an overhead squat; teaches the lockout position
5. **Drop snatch** — from behind the neck, drop quickly under the bar directly into the overhead squat; introduces the speed of the third pull in isolation
6. **Heaving snatch balance** — from behind the neck, drive the bar upward with the legs and then drop under; introduces the drive element
7. **Snatch balance** — includes the dip-drive of the legs before the drop; closest to the full third pull

### Step 3: Learning the Third Pull
8. **Tall snatch** — from the fully extended position (on toes, standing tall), drop under the bar with aggressive arm action and foot transition; teaches the third pull specifically
9. **Mid-hang snatch / Scarecrow snatch** — similar, from a slightly bent position

### Step 4: Learning the Second Pull
10. **Muscle snatch** — full extension without squatting under; emphasizes the pull and turnover mechanics
11. **Power snatch from hang** — snatch from the hang position (above the knee); introduces the combination of second pull and partial third pull

### Step 5: Learning the First Pull
12. **Snatch segment deadlift** — paused pull at key positions (knee, mid-thigh, extension) to develop positional awareness
13. **Snatch deadlift** — full deadlift at snatch grip to strengthen the first pull pattern
14. **Snatch from the floor** — full lift

### Teaching priorities at each step:
- Receiving position must be established before any pulling work
- Do not advance to the next step until the current one is consistent
- Drill volume should be high — many repetitions with light or no load is more valuable than heavy, technically imprecise work

## Pulling from the Floor

**The first pull — key mechanics:**

**Initial movement:** The bar should leave the floor as a result of leg extension — the back angle should change minimally in the early first pull. The "legs-first" initiation is critical.

**Bar path:** The bar should travel as vertically as possible and as close to the body as possible. Any horizontal deviation wastes energy.

**Maintaining the back:**
- The thoracic and lumbar spine must remain rigid throughout — any rounding is unacceptable in the lower back
- The upper back can have a slightly rounded appearance in some athletes due to proportions, but this must not allow rounding of the lumbar spine

**Contact with the body:**
- The bar should remain in contact with or extremely close to the body from liftoff through the second pull
- In the snatch, the bar contacts the thighs just above the knees during the first pull, then makes contact at the hips during the explosion

**Common first pull errors:**
- Bar shifting forward (away from the legs) — typically caused by the weight being too far forward on the feet at liftoff, or shoulders lifting before the hips
- Hips rising faster than the shoulders (good morning) — the back angle becomes too horizontal too early, converting the pull into a deadlift-style movement instead of maintaining the proper leg-dominant initiation

---

## Understanding the Snatch

**The second pull** is the most important phase — it is where the bar receives the majority of its upward acceleration.

**Explosion criteria:**
- The body must be in the power position (torso approximately vertical, hips under shoulders, knees forward, bar at the hip crease) before the final explosion is initiated
- From the power position: simultaneous, violent extension of the knees, hips, and ankles (plantar flexion)
- The torso remains vertical or moves slightly backward — not aggressively leaning back
- Arms remain straight through the explosion — pulling with the arms before the legs and hips have completed their work is a critical error

**Bar path in the second pull:**
- The bar should travel as close to vertically as possible
- Common errors cause the bar to swing forward in an arc, which reduces height and complicates the third pull

**The third pull** — moving under the bar:
- Immediately after full extension, the lifter pulls themselves down aggressively by pulling with the arms
- The elbows drive up and out, rotating the bar to the overhead position
- The feet transition from pull stance to squat stance simultaneously
- The arms lock out as the lifter reaches the receiving position
- The timing of lockout should be as early as possible — reaching the receiving position with already-locked arms is ideal

**Receiving position:**
- Bar balanced directly over the shoulder blades / base of neck
- Hips below parallel (or as deep as the athlete can reach stably)
- Active overhead position throughout — a passive hold will collapse quickly under load

**Recovery from the snatch:**
- Stand up from the overhead squat position maintaining active overhead structure throughout
- Stabilize any bar movement before attempting to stand
- Do not rush — a controlled recovery is safer and more reliable than a hurried one

# Part III: The Clean

## The Rack Position

**The rack position** is where the bar rests on the shoulders during the clean and before the jerk. It is critical for both safety and jerk performance.

**Correct rack position:**
- Bar rests on the front of the shoulders (anterior deltoids) and across the clavicles
- Elbows elevated as high as possible — ideally parallel to the floor or above
- Bar held lightly (fingertip grip or just enough to maintain position) — a full grip is ideal if shoulder mobility allows
- Upper arms approximately parallel to the floor when viewed from the front
- Head neutral and slightly back to create clearance for the bar

**Why elbows must be high:**
- Low elbows allow the bar to roll forward off the shoulders — the higher the elbows, the more secure the shelf
- Low elbows also encourage a forward torso lean, which complicates the jerk

**Grip in the rack:**
- Athletes with sufficient shoulder mobility can maintain a full grip
- Athletes with limited mobility (very common) will use a fingertip grip (2–4 fingers under the bar)
- A false grip (no fingers under) should be avoided — too insecure for heavy cleans and the jerk

---

## Learning the Clean — Progression

The clean learning progression mirrors the snatch:

### Step 1: Receiving Position Work
1. **Grip placement** — establish correct clean grip width (narrower than snatch, determined differently)
2. **Rack position** — establish correct elbow height and shoulder position
3. **Front squat** — full front squat from the rack; establish bottom position before any pulling work

### Step 2: Third Pull Work
4. **Mid-hang clean jump** — from the hang position, emphasis on pulling the body under and establishing the rack position
5. **Tall muscle clean** — from fully extended (on toes), rotating the bar to the rack position
6. **Tall clean** — from fully extended, drop under to the rack position (analogous to tall snatch)

### Step 3: Second and First Pull
7. **Muscle clean** — full extension to rack position without squatting under
8. **Power clean from hang** — clean from hang with partial squat receiving position
9. **Clean segment deadlift** — paused positions
10. **Clean deadlift** — full deadlift at clean grip
11. **Clean from the floor** — full lift

**Missing cleans safely:** Athletes must learn to safely bail from a failed clean — primarily by stepping back and allowing the bar to fall forward, or by quickly releasing the rack and moving backward. This should be practiced explicitly.

---

## Pulling from the Floor (Clean)

**The clean pull mechanics are identical to the snatch pull** with the following differences:

- **Grip width:** Narrower (clean grip), which changes the arm geometry and the contact point of the bar on the body
- **Bar contact point:** The bar contacts the body at approximately the upper thigh or lower hip — not the hip crease as in the snatch. This occurs because the narrower grip shifts the contact point lower.
- **Starting position:** Due to the narrower grip, the starting position may be slightly more upright or slightly different knee angles

**Knee and elbow clearance:**
- With the clean (narrow) grip, the elbows must be turned out to create clearance for the knees during the first pull
- Failure to rotate the elbows sufficiently causes the elbows to contact the knees, disrupting the pull

---

## Understanding the Clean

**The clean second pull** is executed identically to the snatch:
- From the power position, violent simultaneous extension of knees, hips, and ankles
- Arms remain straight through the extension
- Bar remains close to the body

**Bar contact in the second pull:**
- In the clean, the bar contacts the body at the upper thigh, not the hip crease
- This slightly lower contact point is normal for the clean and should not be confused with a fault

**The third pull / turnover (clean):**
- After full extension, the lifter drops under the bar
- The elbows drive forward and upward, rotating the bar into the rack position
- Feet transition from pull stance to squat stance
- The lifter receives the bar in a front squat position

**Receiving position:**
- Full depth front squat (hip crease below top of knee) with high elbows
- Bar secure on the rack (front of shoulders, high elbows)
- Active stabilization of the position before standing

**Recovery:**
- Stand from the front squat with the bar in the rack — identical to a front squat recovery
- Maintain high elbows throughout the recovery
- Do not let elbows drop while standing, as this risks losing the rack and dropping the bar

---

# Part IV: The Jerk

## The Receiving Position (Jerk)

**The jerk receiving position** (typically the split) must be established and trained separately from the jerk itself.

**Overhead position (jerk) vs. overhead position (snatch):**
- The overhead position is identical in structure — bar over the base of the neck, active shoulder retraction, locked elbows
- The difference is the body position below — split position vs. squat position

## The Split Position

**The split position criteria:**

- **Front foot:** Flat on the floor, knee tracking over the toes, shin perpendicular to the floor (ideally) or slightly angled forward
- **Back foot:** Ball of foot in contact with the floor, heel up; knee bent, generally above the floor or close to it
- **Trunk:** Vertical — not leaning forward or backward
- **Hips:** Under the barbell — a direct vertical line from bar to hips to between the feet
- **Foot alignment:** Both feet in line (or close to it) along the line of the lift — not toed out to the side

**Stance width (front-to-back):** Must be long enough to allow the trunk to be vertical, but not so long that recovering is impossible. For most athletes this is approximately shoulder-width to slightly wider.

**Lateral width:** Feet should be approximately hip-width apart laterally — wider reduces stability, narrower increases difficulty of balancing in the split.

---

## Learning the Jerk — Progression

### Step 1: Overhead Mechanics
1. **Jerk overhead position** — establish the correct shoulder, elbow, and wrist position for the jerk overhead
2. **Press behind the neck** — strict press from behind the neck in the jerk grip to establish overhead strength

### Step 2: Split Position
3. **Jump to split** — practice the foot transition from jerk stance to split position without the bar; establish consistent split position
4. **Split position holds** — static holds in the split position to build stability and positional awareness

### Step 3: Jerk Mechanics Without Drive
5. **Push press** — driving the bar from the rack with leg drive but not splitting; teaches the drive mechanics and builds overhead pressing strength
6. **Power jerk** — full leg drive with a squat receiving position instead of split; teaches the full jerk drive with a simpler receiving position
7. **Tall jerk / push under** — from the fully extended position, push the bar overhead while dropping into the split; teaches the push-under in isolation

### Step 4: Full Jerk
8. **Split jerk from rack** — full split jerk
9. **Split jerk from the clean** — jerk immediately after the clean

---

## Understanding the Jerk

**The dip:**
- Smooth, controlled descent — not an abrupt or fast dip
- The dip should be straight down, not forward or backward
- Knees push out over the toes (same direction as the feet) — not forward, not inward
- Typical dip depth: approximately 10–15% of the athlete's height
- The bar should not separate from the shoulders during the dip — separation indicates the dip was too fast

**The drive:**
- Immediately and aggressively reverse the dip direction — no pause at the bottom
- Violent leg extension — the legs push the bar upward with maximum force
- The body must be moving upward before any arm press is initiated
- The drive ends when the athlete leaves the floor or is fully extended on the toes

**The push under:**
- As the bar reaches its maximum height (from the leg drive), the lifter pushes against the bar to drive themselves under it
- This is an active push — not passive dropping under — which preserves the bar's height and allows a lower, more secure catch position
- The elbows drive up and out simultaneously as the feet transition to the split

**Recovery from the split:**
- Front foot recovers first — a half-step backward to the position between the split feet
- Then the back foot steps forward to meet the front foot
- The body should be stable before any foot movement
- The bar must be stabilized overhead (no swaying or oscillating) before recovering

---

## The Clean & Jerk

**The transition between clean and jerk:**
- After receiving the clean, stand to full extension with the bar in the rack
- Establish the jerk starting position: feet adjusted to hip-width jerk stance, weight over the midfoot
- Take a breath, brace the trunk, and initiate the jerk
- The time between standing from the clean and initiating the jerk should be minimal but the athlete must not rush at the expense of correct position

**Grip width adjustment:**
- Some athletes adjust their grip width slightly wider for the jerk compared to the clean
- A slightly wider grip can improve the overhead position by improving the angle of the arms
- This adjustment should be practiced until it becomes automatic and fast

---

# Part V: Error Correction

## Introduction to Error Correction

**Core principle:** Understanding lift mechanics is the foundation of error correction. A coach who understands the principles can devise corrective strategies for any error; a coach relying only on memorized corrections is limited to problems they've seen before.

**Where to start:**
1. Identify the core errors (present consistently) vs. peripheral errors (variable, often caused by core errors)
2. Correct errors in order of occurrence — earlier errors cause later errors
3. Do not spend significant effort on peripheral errors until core errors are addressed

**How to correct:**
- Keep instructions brief and practical during training
- Theory and rationale can be discussed outside of training sessions
- Responses to coaching cues vary dramatically — what works for one athlete may be ineffective for another
- The learning drills from the teaching progressions are also the primary corrective tools — they teach specific elements in isolation

---

## Universal Errors

Errors that appear in both the snatch and the clean:

### Balance Too Far Forward
**Symptom:** Weight on the toes at the start; bar swings forward away from the legs during the first pull; athlete jumps forward under the bar.  
**Cause:** Weight too far forward on the feet in the starting position; shoulders too far in front of the bar.  
**Correction:** Shift the balance back to include the heels before the lift; shift the shoulders over or slightly behind the bar. *Drills: halting snatch/clean deadlift with pauses at key positions; smooth liftoff focus.*

### Hips Rising Faster Than the Shoulders (Early Hip Rise / Good Morning)
**Symptom:** The back angle increases (torso becomes more horizontal) immediately after liftoff; the lift resembles a Romanian deadlift.  
**Cause:** Using the back too early; insufficient leg strength; improper starting position with hips too high.  
**Correction:** Focus on "pushing the floor away" rather than pulling with the back; simultaneous hip and shoulder rise. *Drills: halting deadlift focusing on maintaining back angle; pause at the knee.*

### Bar Drifting Forward (Away from the Body)
**Symptom:** The bar swings away from the legs during the pull, creating a large arc.  
**Cause:** Shoulders too far forward of the bar; insufficient engagement of the lats to keep the bar close; too much pulling with the arms early.  
**Correction:** Maintain shoulders over the bar; actively engage lats by "bending the bar around the legs." *Drills: halting deadlift focusing on bar proximity; snatch/clean from blocks at various heights.*

### Poor Timing of the Second Pull
**Symptom:** The final explosion occurs too early (bar too low on the thighs) or too late (bar passing the hips before explosion).  
**Cause:** Rushing; impatience; poor positional awareness.  
**Correction:** Initiate the explosion when the bar reaches mid-thigh/hip crease; be patient through the first pull. *Drills: snatch/clean pull with pause at the power position; dip snatch/clean.*

### Pulling with the Arms Too Early
**Symptom:** Arms bend before the full extension of the knees and hips; bar path is disrupted; height is reduced.  
**Cause:** Attempting to add height by arm-pulling; lack of trust in leg/hip power; poor timing.  
**Correction:** Arms remain straight until the body is fully extended; focus on "shrugging" not "pulling." *Drills: muscle snatch/clean; snatch/clean pull.*

---

## Snatch Errors

### Missing Overhead (Forward)
**Symptom:** The bar travels forward during the third pull and the athlete cannot receive it, or receives it with the bar well in front of the head.  
**Cause:** Bar drifts forward during the second pull; insufficient pull in the third pull; balance too far forward at extension.  
**Correction:** Keep bar close to the body through the pull; actively engage the arms in the third pull. *Drills: drop snatch; snatch from blocks focusing on bar path.*

### Missing Overhead (Behind)
**Symptom:** The bar travels too far backward and the athlete cannot stabilize it, or receives it behind the head.  
**Cause:** Excessive backward lean at the top of the extension; pulling the bar backward instead of upward; aggressive hip drive that pushes bar backward.  
**Correction:** Maintain more vertical torso at extension; pull the bar upward, not backward. *Drills: pressing snatch balance; overhead squat with slow eccentric to improve stability.*

### Failure to Reach Full Depth
**Symptom:** The athlete catches the snatch in a partial squat (power position), unable to hold it overhead in a full squat.  
**Cause:** Insufficient pulling height; poor timing of third pull; mobility limitations; fear of the full depth.  
**Correction:** Improve pull height and timing; practice receiving at progressively lower positions. *Drills: drop snatch; snatch balance.*

### Soft Elbows Overhead
**Symptom:** Elbows are not fully locked out in the overhead position; the bar sits unstably.  
**Cause:** Tight grip; insufficient strength; poor timing of lockout.  
**Correction:** Relax grip overhead; improve lockout speed through targeted drills. *Drills: overhead squat; snatch balance with emphasis on lockout.*

---

## Clean Errors

### Bar Crashing on the Shoulders
**Symptom:** The bar falls heavily onto the rack position; the athlete's knees buckle or the bar bounces off the shoulders.  
**Cause:** Not pulling the body under actively enough; receiving too high (partial squat) when unable to support the load at that height.  
**Correction:** Active third pull — drive under the bar aggressively. *Drills: muscle clean; tall clean; 1¼ front squat for rack strength.*

### Low Elbows in the Rack Position
**Symptom:** Elbows hang low; the bar rolls forward off the shoulders; the lifter must grip the bar tightly to prevent it from falling.  
**Cause:** Limited shoulder mobility; habit; insufficient practice of the rack position.  
**Correction:** Systematic shoulder mobility work; high-elbow front squat practice. *Drills: front squat with emphasis on elbow height; 1¼ front squat.*

### Over-Gripping in the Rack
**Symptom:** The athlete grips the bar so tightly that the elbows cannot rise adequately.  
**Cause:** Fear of the bar rolling; compensating for insufficient rack position.  
**Correction:** Loosen grip — accept the fingertip or false grip; focus on the shelf created by the shoulders rather than grip. *Drills: front squat with deliberate fingertip grip.*

---

## Jerk Errors

### Forward Dip (Bar Shifting Forward During Dip)
**Symptom:** The bar moves forward away from the shoulders during the dip; the athlete must push the bar backward during the drive.  
**Cause:** Dipping with the hips backward rather than straight down; insufficient upper back tension; dipping too fast.  
**Correction:** Straight-down dip; maintain upper back tension; maintain elbow height during dip. *Drills: jerk dip squats; push press + jerk.*

### Separation of the Bar from the Shoulders
**Symptom:** The bar leaves contact with the shoulders during the dip.  
**Cause:** Dipping too fast; bar oscillation from previous errors.  
**Correction:** Slow down the dip; use heavier jerk dip squats to build tolerance to the load. *Drills: jerk rack support with dip practice.*

### Poor Split Position
**Symptom:** Trunk not vertical in the split; front shin too inclined; back knee dragging on the floor; hips not under the bar.  
**Cause:** Insufficient practice of the split position; rushing; inadequate hip flexibility; habitual errors.  
**Correction:** Practice split position independently; correct each element in isolation. *Drills: jump to split; push jerk behind the neck in split.*

### Pressing Rather Than Pushing Under
**Symptom:** The athlete tries to press the bar overhead rather than pushing the body under it; bar height is insufficient; slow, grinding lockout.  
**Cause:** Insufficient leg drive; distrust of the drive phase; poor sequencing.  
**Correction:** The arms must not press until after the legs have delivered maximum drive; focus on pushing the body under the bar. *Drills: tall jerk; jerk drive (drive only, no split).*

### Hips Behind the Bar in the Split
**Symptom:** The hips are behind the vertical line of the barbell; the athlete leans forward excessively.  
**Cause:** Front foot steps too far forward; dip was too forward.  
**Correction:** Adjust front foot placement; focus on driving straight up rather than forward. *Drills: jump to split focusing on foot position; back squat jump.*

# Part VI: Program Design & Training

## Strength & Power Principles

**Progressive overload:** The most basic principle of all physical training. The body adapts to stress in order to survive; once adapted, it will not continue adapting to the same stress. To stimulate further progress, the magnitude or nature of the stress must continually be increased.

**Biological margin:** The body adapts somewhat beyond the actual imposed demands. This is how an athlete can eventually lift a weight they have never lifted previously. Without natural overcompensation, progress would be impossible.

**Models of adaptation:**

### General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)
Three stages defined by Hans Selye:
- **Alarm:** Immediate response to a stressor — performance diminishes, soreness, reduced speed, power, and strength
- **Resistance (Adaptation):** The body makes cellular, structural, and neurological changes to better handle the stress — this period varies in duration based on training history, magnitude of stress, genetics, and restoration effort
- **Exhaustion:** If stress continues too long without adequate recovery, the body's adaptive capacity is overwhelmed — performance drops back below baseline and remains there

### Fitness-Fatigue Model
- Training produces both fitness (adaptation) and fatigue (temporary performance reduction)
- The net performance at any given point is the result of fitness minus fatigue
- Fatigue dissipates faster than fitness accumulates
- Proper programming exploits this by planning periods of high fatigue (heavy training) followed by periods of reduced fatigue (taper) — allowing the underlying fitness to express itself

### Supercompensation Model
- After a training stimulus and adequate recovery, the body overcompensates beyond its previous baseline
- The window of peak performance (supercompensation) is temporary — without further stimulus it returns to baseline
- Training must be timed to apply new stimuli during or near the supercompensation window

**Specificity:** Adaptations are specific to the type of training performed. Weightlifting performance is improved most by weightlifting training and closely related exercises. General fitness work has limited transfer.

**Power:** Power is force applied rapidly (force × velocity). Weightlifting is fundamentally a power sport. Developing maximal strength is necessary but not sufficient — that strength must be expressed at high velocity.

---

## Assessment

**Before designing any program, the coach must assess the athlete across multiple dimensions:**

### Physical Traits
- **Strength:** Current levels in competition lifts, squats, presses — both absolute and relative to bodyweight
- **Speed and power:** Ability to generate force rapidly — observable in the lifts but also measurable through jump testing
- **Mobility:** Ankle, hip, thoracic, and shoulder mobility — all directly affect position and technique
- **Structural:** Body proportions — femur length, torso length, arm length — affect how the lifts will look and what modifications are needed
- **Recovery ability:** Some athletes recover exceptionally well and can handle more volume; others are more sensitive

### Circumstances
- Training history and experience level
- Number of years training weightlifting specifically
- Competition goals and timeline
- Available training time per week (days and sessions per day)
- Life stress (work, school, family) — which reduces recovery capacity
- Training environment (equipment, coaching access)

### Skill Level Assessment
The book provides a skill level framework (0–7) based on technical proficiency and competitive performance:
- **Level 0:** Complete beginner — no experience with Olympic lifts
- **Level 1:** Familiar with the movements but limited technique
- **Level 2:** Basic technique established, training consistently
- **Level 3:** Competing locally, technique improving
- **Level 4–5:** Regional/national-level competitors, refined technique
- **Level 6–7:** Elite national/international competitors

The appropriate program volume, intensity, and exercise selection differs significantly across these levels. Beginning athletes make rapid progress with relatively low volumes and should not be given programs designed for advanced athletes.

---

## Training Variables

### Intensity
**Definition:** The weight used in a given exercise.
- **Absolute intensity:** Actual weight in kilograms (e.g., 120 kg)
- **Relative intensity:** Percentage of the athlete's maximum (e.g., 86% of 140 kg max)
- **Objective intensity:** Specific numerical prescription based on training or competition maxes
- **Subjective intensity:** Qualitative prescription (heavy, moderate, light, maximal) — particularly useful for beginners whose maxes are rapidly changing

**Intensity modulation:** Intensity must vary systematically — sustained high intensity leads to accumulated fatigue and eventual performance decline. Variation creates the conditions for supercompensation.

### Volume
**Definition:** The total amount of work performed — measured in total repetitions (most commonly), total kilograms lifted, or other metrics.

**Volume and intensity relationship:**
- Volume and intensity are inversely related within any given period — high intensity requires lower volume; high volume requires lower intensity
- Both must vary cyclically to produce progressive adaptation

**Calculating volume:** Total reps = sets × reps per set (summed across all exercises and weights). Tonnage = sum of (weight × sets × reps) for each exercise.

**Relative volume:** The volume relative to the athlete's maximal capacity — the same 10 sets may be low volume for an advanced athlete and overwhelming for a beginner.

### Repetitions
**Rep ranges by training purpose:**

| Purpose | Rep Range |
|---|---|
| Maximal strength/competition practice | 1–2 |
| Strength/technique at high intensity | 2–3 |
| Moderate intensity technique/strength | 3–5 |
| Volume/technique development | 5+ (for non-competition lifts) |

For the competition lifts (snatch, clean & jerk), singles and doubles dominate advanced training. Higher rep sets are more common in early training stages and for supplemental exercises.

### Interset Rest
- **Competition lifts:** 2–5+ minutes depending on intensity; maximal attempts require full neural recovery
- **Supplemental/strength work:** 2–3 minutes typically
- **Technique drills:** 1–2 minutes; the goal is movement quality, not metabolic stress

Rest periods should be consistent and deliberate — guessing or minimizing rest without purpose leads to unpredictable results.

### Exercise Selection
- **Primary exercises:** Snatch, clean & jerk — the competition lifts and their direct derivatives
- **Strength exercises:** Back squat, front squat, overhead squat, presses
- **Pulling exercises:** Snatch pulls, clean pulls, and their variations — maintain pulling mechanics while developing strength
- **Technique exercises:** Segment lifts, pause lifts, positional work — used to address specific technical deficiencies
- **Accessory work:** Exercises targeting specific weaknesses or imbalances — used to support the primary training without replacing it

---

## Jump Training

Jump training develops explosive power that directly transfers to the second pull of the snatch and clean.

**Types:**
- **Vertical jumps:** Direct measure and development of vertical power
- **Box jumps:** Jump onto a box; develops power with a lower landing impact
- **Depth jumps:** Step off a box and jump immediately upon landing; develops reactive strength (stretch-shortening cycle)
- **Broad jumps:** Horizontal power development

**Programming jump training:**
- Best placed at the beginning of a session when the athlete is fresh — before fatiguing strength or skill work
- Volume should be moderate — jump training is high-intensity by nature
- Do not combine with high volumes of squatting in the same session
- Particularly valuable for beginner and intermediate athletes developing their power base; less necessary for advanced athletes whose training provides sufficient power stimulus

---

## Accessory Work

Accessory exercises serve to address imbalances, specific weaknesses, and complementary physical qualities without replacing the primary training.

**Categories:**
- **Trunk/core:** Good mornings, back extensions, GHD sit-ups, planks — to develop the spinal erectors and trunk musculature that support the pulls
- **Upper back:** Rows, face pulls, band pull-aparts — address the posterior chain of the upper body important for rack position and overhead stability
- **Single-leg:** Romanian deadlifts (single leg), Bulgarian split squats — address strength imbalances between legs
- **Pressing:** Strict press, push press — develops overhead pressing strength for the jerk

**Programming principles:**
- Accessory work should be placed after primary training, not before
- Volume should be conservative enough not to compromise recovery for the primary lifts
- Exercises should be directly relevant to the athlete's needs — assess before prescribing

---

## The Bulgarian Method

The Bulgarian method refers to the approach developed by Ivan Abadjiev during the period of Bulgarian dominance in weightlifting (1970s–90s).

**Core characteristics:**
- Maximal frequency: training daily (typically twice daily) with the competition lifts
- Maximal intensity: training at or near the athlete's maximum on the competition lifts every session
- Minimal exercise variety: focuses primarily on snatch, clean & jerk, front squat, and their power variations
- Progressive overload through accumulation: the volume of maximal effort training accumulates over time

**Why it can work:**
- The body adapts specifically to the demands placed on it — training at near-maximal intensity regularly eventually makes near-maximal efforts routine
- Fatigue is managed through the selection of exercises (no pulling exercises that would cause excessive structural fatigue) and by the athlete's adaptation over time

**Why it is inappropriate for most athletes:**
- Requires genetic capacity for recovery that is not common
- Requires significant prior preparation and technical mastery before it can be applied
- The dropout rate in the Bulgarian system was extremely high — the athletes who succeeded were the rare individuals suited to this approach
- Without the genetic recovery ability, the method leads rapidly to overtraining and injury

**Modified Bulgarian approaches:** Various coaches use elements of the Bulgarian method (higher frequency of competition lifts, emphasis on subjective maximal effort) without the extreme intensity prescription. These modified versions can be effective for a wider range of athletes.

---

## Specific Populations

### Women
- Adapt as well as men to weightlifting training
- Typically require somewhat less volume initially due to different recovery profiles, but adapt to higher volumes
- The same technical and programming principles apply
- Women are generally more technically coachable and consistent in early training

### Masters Athletes (35+)
- Recovery requires more time — training frequency and weekly volume may need reduction
- Injury prevention becomes more critical — mobility work and conservative loading progressions
- Progress is slower but absolutely possible; many masters athletes compete successfully at national and international levels
- Focus on technical precision and moderate, consistent volume rather than maximal loading

### Youth Athletes
- Technique, general physical development, and enjoyment are the priorities
- Heavy loading should be delayed until structural maturity
- Excellent technique established early provides a foundation for a lifetime of lifting
- Long-term athlete development (LTAD) principles should guide youth programming

### Beginning Athletes
- Make rapid progress regardless of program — most beginners are "program-proof"
- Focus on technique above all else
- Volume should be moderate — the athlete will make more progress from consistent technical work than from excessive loading
- The goal in the first year is to establish all basic positions and movement patterns

---

## The Program Design Process

### Step 1: Assess the Athlete
Determine skill level, physical traits, circumstances, and goals.

### Step 2: Establish a Timeline
Work backward from goal competitions to establish training cycles.

### Step 3: Determine Macrocycle Structure
A macrocycle is the longest training block — typically one training year or a period ending at a major competition. Typically divided into:
- **Preparatory period:** Higher volume, lower average intensity; emphasis on building physical qualities and addressing technical deficiencies
- **Pre-competitive period:** Transitioning from volume to intensity; competition-specific technical work
- **Competitive period:** Peak intensity, reduced volume; competition preparation and competition itself
- **Transition/recovery period:** Active recovery; lower intensity and volume; physical and mental restoration

### Step 4: Plan Mesocycles
A mesocycle is a 3–6 week training block with a specific emphasis (volume, intensity, or transition). Within each mesocycle, the coach plans:
- Primary exercises for that block
- Volume targets (total reps per week/mesocycle)
- Intensity ranges
- Key training sessions (heavy testing sessions, competition simulation)

### Step 5: Plan Microcycles
A microcycle is a single training week. Plan day-by-day:
- Highest intensity work on the days when the athlete is freshest
- Session sequencing (technique first, strength second in multi-session days)
- Distribution of volume across the week

### Step 6: Intensity Prescriptions
Methods of prescribing intensity:
- **Percentage-based:** Prescribe specific percentages of the athlete's best lift (e.g., 80% × 3 × 5)
- **RPE-based:** Prescribe based on effort level (e.g., "a weight that is an 8/10 effort for 3 reps")
- **Heavy single / Heavy double:** The athlete works up to the heaviest weight they can manage for that number of reps on that day

---

## Restoration & Recovery

**Foundational principle:** Training cannot be effective in the absence of adequate restoration. Too many coaches over-invest in planning training and under-invest in planning recovery.

### Sleep
**The absolute first priority.** Sleep is the period of most profound physical and mental recuperation.
- Quantity and quality both matter
- Structure the day so a specific number of hours of sleep are possible — treat sleep scheduling with the same priority as training scheduling
- Maintain a consistent sleep and wake schedule
- Wind down 2+ hours before bed — no screens immediately before sleep
- Training should end more than 2 hours before bedtime
- Develop a consistent pre-bed routine
- Black-out curtains, cool room temperature, and elimination of electronic light sources improve sleep quality

### Nutrition for Recovery
- Adequate protein to support muscle repair and growth (generally 1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight for strength athletes)
- Adequate carbohydrates to replenish muscle glycogen between sessions
- Post-training nutrition (protein + carbohydrate) within 30–60 minutes of training
- Hydration — dehydration impairs performance and recovery
- Micronutrients (vitamins, minerals) through diverse food intake

### Active Recovery
- Rest days should include light movement — not complete sedentarism
- Dynamic range of motion exercises, walking, light swimming or cycling at very low intensity
- Static stretching and self-myofascial release on rest days
- The goal is to maintain blood flow, reduce stiffness, and support neurological recovery without creating new fatigue

### Restorative Modalities
- **Contrast therapy (hot/cold):** Alternating hot (sauna or hot tub) and cold (cold plunge or ice bath) exposure; effective for reducing muscular soreness and fatigue
- **Massage:** Professional or self-massage (foam rolling, lacrosse ball) to reduce tissue restriction and soreness
- **Sauna:** Promotes recovery through circulatory effects and relaxation
- **Compression:** Compression garments can reduce muscular soreness between sessions

### Mental Recovery
- Training creates psychological as well as physical stress
- Athletes should have activities outside of lifting that provide genuine mental relaxation
- Coaches should be aware of signs of mental burnout — loss of motivation, anxiety about training, irritability
- Periodically reduced training loads serve mental as well as physical recovery

---

## Training Practices

### Barbell Loading Protocol
- Always use collars on the bar
- Load plates symmetrically — add and remove plates from both sides simultaneously
- Learn the standard plate denominations (25, 15, 10, 5, 2.5, 1.25 kg) and combinations for efficient loading
- When incrementing up in weight, remove smaller plates and add larger ones — not simply add more plates

### Session Structure
1. Warm-up (SMR, DROMs, technique primer)
2. Technical work (snatch or clean & jerk practice at moderate intensity)
3. Strength work (squats, pulls)
4. Accessory work
5. Cool-down (stretching, SMR)

**For double sessions:**
- AM session: strength-focused (squats, pulls, pressing) — the nervous system is not at peak excitability early in the morning
- PM session: technical lifts (snatch, clean & jerk) — peak excitability in the late afternoon/early evening

### Training Log
Keep detailed records of:
- Date and session
- Exercises, sets, reps, weights
- Notes on technical performance and feel
- Bodyweight
- Sleep quality (subjective)
- Recovery status

A training log is essential for tracking progress, identifying patterns, and informing future programming decisions.

# Part VII: Supplemental Exercises

The supplemental exercises section of the book contains information on a wide array of training exercises — technique-oriented lifts, strength lifts, and many that serve both purposes.

## Snatch Exercises (Selected Key Exercises)

**Snatch Deadlift:** A snatch-grip deadlift from the floor to full extension. Used to develop strength and positional awareness in the snatch starting position and first pull. Typically loaded heavier than the snatch (100–120%+ of snatch max).

**Snatch Segment Deadlift (Halting Snatch Deadlift):** A snatch deadlift paused at one or more specific positions (below the knee, at the knee, mid-thigh, power position). Develops positional strength and awareness at each segment.

**Snatch Pull:** A snatch from the floor through the full extension without squatting under the bar. Develops the timing and power of the second pull. Typically loaded at 90–110%+ of snatch max.

**Block Snatch / Snatch from Blocks:** Snatch performed from pulling blocks at various heights, eliminating the first pull to focus on the second pull and third pull. Useful for athletes with first-pull problems.

**Hang Snatch:** Snatch from the hang position (above the floor) at various heights — above the knee, at the knee, or below the knee. Develops the second pull timing and position.

**Power Snatch:** Snatch caught in a partial squat (hip crease above the top of the knee). Develops the pull and turnover while demanding more height. Often used as a training tool and in lighter preparation sessions.

**Muscle Snatch:** From the full extension (or hang), the bar is pulled to the overhead position without any squat or transition — purely using arm and shoulder strength. Develops the turnover mechanics and upper body pulling strength.

**Drop Snatch:** From behind the neck, the athlete drops quickly into the full overhead squat receiving position without any drive. Used to develop the speed and confidence of the third pull and receiving position.

**Snatch Balance:** Same as drop snatch but with a dip-drive before the drop. More closely mirrors the full jerk mechanics in the context of the snatch receiving position.

**Overhead Squat:** Full squat with the bar held overhead in the snatch grip. Develops mobility, stability, and strength in the snatch receiving position.

**Snatch Push Press (Behind the Neck):** Strict press from behind the neck in the snatch grip — develops overhead pressing strength specific to the snatch overhead position.

**Snatch Press (Sotts Press):** Overhead press from the bottom of the squat position — extremely demanding of mobility; develops upper body strength and stability in the deep receiving position.

## Clean Exercises (Selected Key Exercises)

**Clean Deadlift:** Clean-grip deadlift from the floor to full extension. Develops strength and position in the clean first pull.

**Clean Segment Deadlift:** Paused clean deadlift at key positions. Same purpose as the snatch segment deadlift.

**Clean Pull:** From the floor, full extension without squatting under. Develops timing and power of the clean second pull. Loaded at 90–110%+ of clean max.

**Power Clean:** Clean caught in a partial squat. Develops the pull and turnover while demanding more bar height.

**Hang Clean:** Clean from the hang at various heights.

**Muscle Clean:** Clean to rack position without squatting under — develops the turnover mechanics.

**Front Squat:** Fundamental strength builder for the clean receiving position. Should be trained consistently and heavily.

**1¼ Front Squat:** Front squat that descends to the bottom, rises a quarter of the way, descends again, then rises fully. Develops strength in the most demanding range of motion at the bottom of the receiving position.

**Clean-Grip Romanian Deadlift:** Develops hamstring and spinal erector strength in the clean pulling pattern.

## Jerk Exercises (Selected Key Exercises)

**Push Press:** Bar from the jerk rack position; leg drive used to initiate the press; arms complete the press overhead. Develops leg drive and overhead pressing strength for the jerk.

**Jerk Dip Squat:** Jerk rack position; perform the dip only (bending and straightening the knees) without pressing the bar overhead. Develops dip mechanics and loading tolerance.

**Power Jerk:** Full leg drive with a squat receiving position instead of split. Develops the drive and push-under mechanics.

**Split Jerk Variations:**
- Jerk from blocks (no clean required)
- Push jerk behind the neck in the split position — develops split position stability under load
- Jerk recovery — standing from the split only, without the drive portion

**Press Behind the Neck:** Strict press from behind the neck in the jerk grip. Develops overhead pressing strength for the jerk.

## General Exercises (Selected)

**Back Squat:** The primary strength builder for weightlifting. Should be trained twice weekly in most programs.

**Good Morning:** Hip hinge with bar on the back — develops spinal erectors and hamstrings. Load conservatively; never to failure.

**Romanian Deadlift (RDL):** Develops hamstrings and spinal erectors; lower injury risk than conventional deadlift.

**Bulgarian Split Squat:** Single-leg squat with rear foot elevated. Addresses leg strength imbalances.

**Glute-Ham Raise:** Develops the hamstrings and spinal erectors in the range of motion most relevant to the first pull.

**Stiff-Legged Deadlift:** Hamstring and lower back development; loaded carefully.

**Complexes:** Multiple exercises combined into a single set (e.g., Snatch Pull + Snatch + Overhead Squat = 1+1+1). Provides specific combinations of strength and technique work. Always listed in order of execution.

# Part VIII: Nutrition & Bodyweight

## Nutrition Principles

**Macronutrient fundamentals:**
- **Protein:** Primary building block for muscle repair and growth. Target approximately 1.6–2.2 g/kg bodyweight for strength athletes. Spread across meals.
- **Carbohydrates:** Primary fuel source for explosive exercise. Should constitute a significant portion of caloric intake for weightlifters. Prioritize around training.
- **Fat:** Essential for hormonal function, joint health, and sustained energy. Should not be eliminated or minimized excessively.
- **Calories:** Total caloric intake must match the goal — weight gain, weight maintenance, or weight loss — while supporting training performance.

**Post-training nutrition:**
- Consume protein and carbohydrate within 30–60 minutes after training
- This window supports muscle protein synthesis and glycogen replenishment
- A meal rather than a supplement is preferable when practical

**Pre-training nutrition:**
- Avoid training fasted for high-intensity weightlifting sessions
- A moderate meal 2–3 hours before training provides energy without digestive discomfort

**Hydration:**
- Dehydration impairs performance significantly at even modest levels (2% body mass)
- Drink consistently throughout the day — not just around training
- Replace fluids and electrolytes after training, especially in hot environments

## Bodyweight Management

**Weight class strategy:**
- Athletes should train and compete at the highest bodyweight within their class
- The goal is to maximize lean muscle mass within the class limits
- Dramatic weight cutting (water cutting) to compete in a lighter class is common at higher levels but carries health risks and should be managed carefully

**Weight gain:**
- A controlled surplus of calories (200–500 kcal above maintenance) supports muscle gain with minimal fat accumulation
- Expect 0.5–1 kg of bodyweight gain per month at most

**Weight loss:**
- Minimize fat loss during training periods — caloric restriction impairs recovery and performance
- If weight loss is necessary, do it during lower-intensity training periods
- Avoid losing more than 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week to minimize muscle loss

**Body composition vs. bodyweight:**
- Total bodyweight is less important than body composition (ratio of lean mass to fat)
- An athlete who reduces fat while maintaining muscle mass will perform better even if the scale doesn't move

## Supplements

**Generally useful:**
- **Creatine monohydrate:** The most research-supported performance supplement for strength athletes. 3–5 g/day maintains muscle creatine stores. Safe for long-term use.
- **Protein powder:** Convenient for meeting protein targets, not superior to food protein. Whey, casein, and plant-based options all have utility.
- **Caffeine:** Improves focus, strength, and endurance. 3–6 mg/kg bodyweight 45–60 minutes pre-training. Tolerance develops; cycle use.

**Use with caution (limited evidence):**
- Beta-alanine: may improve muscular endurance in longer-duration efforts; limited direct applicability to weightlifting
- HMB: mixed evidence; possibly useful for beginners or deconditioned athletes

**Avoid:**
- Stimulant-heavy pre-workout supplements — high doses of caffeine and other stimulants can impair technique and create dependence
- Any supplement not tested for banned substances (WADA prohibitions) when competing in tested competition

# Part IX: Mobility & Flexibility

## Introduction

Mobility refers to the functional range of motion actively available to an athlete during movement. Flexibility refers to the passive range of motion of a muscle or joint. Both are important, but mobility is more directly relevant to performance.

**Priority areas for weightlifters:**
- **Ankle dorsiflexion:** Allows the shin to move forward over the foot in the squat — essential for full-depth squats with an upright torso
- **Hip flexion and external rotation:** Allows the deep squat with a neutral spine
- **Thoracic extension:** Allows the upright torso in the front squat and clean rack position
- **Shoulder flexion and external rotation:** Allows the snatch overhead position
- **Wrist extension:** Allows the overhead position with the bar in the palm

## Self-Myofascial Release (SMR)

SMR (foam rolling, lacrosse ball work) is performed to address soft-tissue restriction and soreness before and after training.

**Key areas:**
- Thoracic spine (foam roller)
- Quadriceps and hip flexors
- Lats and posterior shoulder
- Calves and Achilles tendon

**How to perform:** Find areas of restriction or tenderness; apply sustained pressure for 30–90 seconds or until the area relaxes. Use slow rolling motion to identify tender spots.

## Key Stretches

**Hook grip stretch:** Extend the thumb and fingers away from the hand to stretch the muscles and connective tissue compressed by the hook grip. Hold 30–60 seconds.

**Leaning bar hang:** Hang from a bar with the arms overhead, leaning slightly back — stretches the lats and shoulder girdle.

**Doorjamb / underarm rack stretch:** Hook the arm at the elbow over a rack or doorjamb; rotate the body away to stretch the anterior shoulder and pec.

**Back squat push through:** In the bottom of the back squat position, use the elbows to push the knees out while sitting up — improves hip external rotation in the deep squat position.

**Sleeper stretch:** Lying on the side, the arm is internally rotated against resistance — improves internal rotation of the shoulder.

**Russian baby maker:** A dynamic squat-based hip mobility drill; squat deeply and use the elbows to push the knees out while maintaining an upright torso.

**Lunge stretch / Spiderman lunge:** Stretches the hip flexors and provides active hip mobility in a functional pattern.

**Pigeon stretch:** Hip external rotation and posterior hip stretch — addresses limitations in the squat receiving position.

**Butterfly stretch:** Hip external rotation and groin stretch.

**Ankle mobility drills:** Wall ankle stretch (foot close to wall, knee over toes) — the most direct way to improve ankle dorsiflexion.

**General principle:** Static stretching is most effective after training or when the body is warm. Before training, use dynamic range of motion movements rather than sustained static stretches, which can temporarily reduce force production.

# Part X: Competition

## Competition Structure

**In the United States and internationally, weightlifting competition follows IWF rules:**
- Each athlete has 3 attempts in the snatch and 3 attempts in the clean & jerk (6 attempts total)
- Only the best successful lift in each counts toward the total
- The athlete with the highest total wins their weight class and age category
- In the event of tied totals, the lighter athlete wins (or the one who achieved the total first, depending on the rule set)
- An athlete who makes no successful snatches is eliminated from the clean & jerk

## Age Categories

| Category | Age |
|---|---|
| Youth 13 & Under | ≤ 13 |
| Youth 14–15 | 14–15 |
| Youth 16–17 | 16–17 |
| Junior | 15–20 (subtract birth year from competition year) |
| University | 15–25 |
| Senior | All ages 15+ |
| Masters | 35+ |

The minimum age for senior, junior, and university world championships is 15. The minimum age for the Olympics is 16.

## Bodyweight Categories (IWF)

**Men (senior/junior):** 55, 61, 67, 73, 81, 89, 96, 102, 109, +109 kg  
**Women (senior/junior):** 45, 49, 55, 59, 64, 71, 76, 81, 87, +87 kg  
*(Note: weight classes have been updated since this edition; verify current IWF weight classes)*

Athletes should train and compete at the heaviest weight within their class — being the lighter of two tied athletes is not a strategy but an occasional bonus.

## Qualifying Totals

Many competitions (regional, national) require athletes to achieve minimum totals in sanctioned competition during a qualifying period. Check specific competition information for current requirements.

## Competition Preparation Training

**12-week competition prep guideline:**
- **Weeks 1–6 (Preparatory):** Higher volume, moderate intensity; technical work, strength building
- **Weeks 7–9 (Pre-competitive):** Transitioning — volume decreasing, intensity increasing; session simulations
- **Weeks 10–12 (Competitive):** Low volume, high intensity; maximal attempts; final competition preparation

**Peaking:**
- Reduce volume significantly in the final 1–2 weeks
- Maintain some intensity to keep the nervous system primed
- The final week should include a light technical session 1–2 days before competition and rest the day before

**Competition simulation in training:**
- Practice the exact competition routine — warm up as you would at a meet, attempt the weights in 3 attempts per lift, rest as you would between sessions

## Competition Procedure

### Weigh-In
- Occurs 2 hours before the session begins
- Athletes must weigh at or under their declared weight class
- At weigh-in, athletes (or their coaches) declare their opening attempts for both the snatch and the clean & jerk
- A national/club ID and competition license are required

### Warm-Up Room
- Athletes warm up in a separate room from the competition platform
- Warm-up must be coordinated with the competition clock — the athlete needs to be ready when called
- The coach tracks the competition and communicates when the athlete needs to take their attempts
- **Rule of thumb:** Take the final warm-up attempt approximately 3–5 attempts before going on the platform

### Attempt Changes
- Opening attempts may be changed before the lifter is called to the platform
- Subsequent attempts must be submitted within 1 minute of the previous attempt being completed
- An athlete may increase or decrease their next attempt, but cannot change it again once submitted, with limited exceptions

### Attempt Order
- Attempts are taken in order of the weight declared — the lightest weight goes first
- An athlete taking consecutive attempts (no one else competing at that or a lighter weight) gets a 2-minute clock rather than the standard 1-minute clock

### Judging
- Three judges evaluate each attempt — the lift is declared good with 2 or more white lights (successful), bad with 2 or more red lights (failed)
- Causes for red lights include: any downward movement of the bar before the finish, pressing out (rebending the elbows overhead), foot movement during the lift (in the jerk, feet must stop moving before the down signal), not waiting for the down signal

### Strategy
**Opening attempt (snatch):** A weight the athlete can lift with high confidence — typically 90–93% of training best. Never open with a PR attempt.

**Second attempt:** Based on the first attempt success — typically 2–5 kg above the opener.

**Third attempt:** Competition PR attempt or strategic total-securing lift.

**Clean & jerk strategy:** The same conservative progression applies. The clean & jerk opening attempt should be achievable even after the exertion of the snatch and the rest period.

**Total strategy:** Sometimes, the objective is to secure a specific total (for a qualifying purpose or to win) rather than maximize the total. This may mean taking a conservative third attempt to secure the total rather than a PR attempt.

**Coaching during competition:**
- The coach communicates with the athlete during warm-up and between attempts
- During the actual attempt, the coach is not permitted on the platform
- Post-attempt, the coach should provide brief, clear feedback — not extensive technical instruction
- The primary role of the coach is to manage the competition — handle attempt declarations, timing, and logistics — so the athlete can focus on lifting

## Glossary (Selected Key Terms)

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Absolute intensity** | The actual weight used in an exercise, in kilograms |
| **Complex** | A series of exercises performed consecutively in a single set |
| **Double knee bend** | The temporary cessation or slight re-bending of the knees during the transition from first to second pull |
| **Drop snatch** | A learning drill in which the athlete drops directly into the overhead squat receiving position from behind the neck without a drive |
| **First pull** | The phase of the snatch/clean from liftoff to approximately mid-thigh |
| **Hook grip** | A pronated grip in which the thumb is trapped between the bar and the first two fingers |
| **Macrocycle** | The longest programmatic training block — typically one year or a major competition cycle |
| **Mesocycle** | A medium-length training block of 3–6 weeks with a specific training emphasis |
| **Microcycle** | A single training week |
| **Power position** | The position at the initiation of the final explosion: torso approximately vertical, knees forward, bar at the hip crease |
| **Progressive overload** | The principle that training stress must continually increase to stimulate continued adaptation |
| **Rack position** | The position of the bar on the shoulders before the jerk (and in the clean receiving position) |
| **Receiving position** | The position in which the lifter catches the bar — overhead squat in the snatch, front squat in the clean, split in the jerk |
| **Relative intensity** | The weight used as a percentage of the athlete's maximum in that exercise |
| **Second pull** | The final explosion phase of the snatch/clean, from mid-thigh through full extension |
| **Supercompensation** | The temporary elevation of performance above baseline that follows adequate recovery from training stress |
| **Third pull** | The athlete's active descent under the bar from the fully extended position to the receiving position |
| **Tonnage** | Total volume measured in kilograms lifted (sets × reps × weight, summed across all exercises) |
| **Transition** | See double knee bend; also refers to the shift of the feet from pull stance to squat stance during the third pull |
