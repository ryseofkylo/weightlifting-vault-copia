## Introduction

This book builds on *Chinese Weightlifting: Technical Mastery and Training* by providing a practical guide to performing the snatch, clean, and jerk using Chinese weightlifting techniques, and how to overcome technique issues to improve your lifting and enhance your coaching.

The text is organized in the following ways:

1. **Technical elements and their importance.** Part of improving technique is knowing what to look for; otherwise it is impossible to know what to fix.
2. **Chinese technical standards and their rationale.** Some methods may differ from what you have learned. The author considers the Chinese style the most effective for amateur and professionals to lift the heaviest weight with the least stress on the body.
3. **Common faults** observed in China and abroad, so you can avoid them in teaching or training. Sometimes a fault results from an earlier problem (e.g., a fault during the catch can result from an incorrect start position).
4. **Internal cues, external cues, and training recommendations** to guide implementation.

> Since the snatch and clean share very similar start positions and deadlifts, there is a single Start Position chapter and Deadlift chapter. The Jerk chapter reviews techniques identical for the split jerk and squat jerk.

## Chinese Weightlifting Philosophy — The 5 Words

The Chinese weightlifting philosophy developed in the late 1960s. It is built on five interrelated principles:

### 1. Close
The barbell must be close to the body, and the body must stay close to the barbell. This keeps the center of gravity of the barbell near the athlete's, reducing resistance and making it easier to lift. The best lifters satisfy this principle on the way up and on the way down.

### 2. Fast
The upward speed of the barbell must increase and be fast. This is often misunderstood — it does not mean pulling forcefully off the ground at the cost of position. The bar should be pushed off the ground smoothly while maintaining position so maximum force transfers into the barbell.

### 3. Low
The barbell should be caught in the lowest position possible while maintaining balance and tension. Maximizing height does not mean pulling as high as possible — it means saving energy, pulling to the minimum necessary height, and letting the barbell's upward momentum do the rest.

### 4. Timing
Coordinating muscles at the right moment creates a distinct rhythm from the moment of extension to the finished catch. A shorter time creates better rhythm. Athletes with excellent timing lift heavy weights while appearing to move smoothly and easily.

### 5. Stable
The athlete should keep balance throughout the lift — not only in the forward-backward (sagittal) direction, but also side-to-side (frontal) and rotational (transverse). Stability comes from satisfying *close, fast, low*, and *timing*. Catching near the barbell's apex reduces downward momentum and stress on joints.

> The five words are interrelated. It is impossible to maximize barbell speed without being close. It is impossible to maximize catchable weight without maximizing speed and height. Without proper timing, it is difficult to catch near the apex in a stable position.

## Start Position

### Stance Width

**Standard:** Hip-width stance for both the snatch and clean. This mimics a natural vertical jump, provides the most power, and allows the athlete to reach the highest extension height. Using the same stance for both lifts means each reinforces the other.

**Fault — Too narrow:** Demands more ankle, hip, and spine flexibility. Most athletes cannot lower properly with this stance.

**Fault — Too wide:** Shorter extension height. Athletes often compensate by leaning back, violating the *close* principle. Catching deep is also more difficult.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Position yourself to jump vertically — observe your natural stance.
- Alternatively, prepare as if about to sprint, then evaluate stance width.

---

### Stance Distance

**Standard:** Stand so foot pressure is firmly on the ball of the foot. This mimics the natural push-off in a vertical jump and minimizes horizontal forces on the barbell. Same distance for snatch and clean.

**Fault — Too close (near ankle):** The barbell must travel forward to the ball of the foot, creating forward momentum. Plantarflexion is reduced.

**Fault — Too far (beyond ball of foot):** Barbell too far from the body, violating *close*. Forces more flexion in ankles, knees, and hips.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Bend over while keeping shins vertical and feet flat, then measure one fist-width from a vertical shin to the barbell.
- Do not squat down to measure; this inclines the shins.
- Use your hand — body proportions vary.

---

### Shin Position

**Standard:** Move shins forward to eliminate the gap with the barbell while keeping pressure on the ball of the foot. This reduces barbell resistance, allows lower body muscles to stay near resting length, and prevents barbell movement during setup.

**Fault — Gap remains with shins forward:** Barbell too far away. Creates backward or forward momentum depending on strength.

**Fault — Gap with barbell over ball of foot:** Shins not inclined enough; balance too far back.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Walk to the bar with feet straight, one fist-width away, then move shins to contact.
- Wear weightlifting shoes.
- Strengthen anterior shin muscles: lunges, narrow squats, step-ups, single-leg squats.

---

### Foot Balance

**Standard:** Flat feet with pressure over the ball of the foot during the deadlift. Flat feet maximize ground contact and stability; pressure on the ball of the foot maximizes vertical jumping power.

**Fault — Balance too far back (hips drop to straighten spine):** Toes may lift. Reduces connection with the floor.

**Fault — Balance too far forward:** Heels may lift. Barbell must travel around shins and knees. Athlete often jumps forward.

**Coaching Tips:**
- If heels rise: see Shin Position section, or shift balance back until feet are flat.
- If toes lift: imagine grabbing the ground with your toes like a tiger, or place tape under the ball of the foot.

---

### Foot & Knee Alignment

**Standard:** Feet turned outward with the midline of each foot aligned with the knee. Equal pressure between the lateral and medial sides. The exact angle depends on hip structure. Glutes contract to rotate the knees out.

**Fault — Knees too far out:** Pressure on the lateral edge of the foot. Reduces adductor contribution and overstresses the posterior and lateral knee.

**Fault — Knees caving in:** Pressure on the medial edge. Reduces glute contribution and overstresses the medial knee.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Draw a straight line from the knee toward the floor and observe whether it is inside or outside the foot.
- Adjust foot angle or knee direction until knee aligns with the midline of the foot.

---

### Lower Back Position

**Standard:** Contract the lower back muscles to maintain an upright torso and the natural lumbar curvature. This keeps the torso close to the barbell and prepares the body to transfer vertical force.

**Fault — Hyperextended lower back:** Stretches the abdominals, overloads vertebrae and lower back, can shift balance toward the toes.

**Fault — Rounded lower back:** Lengthens the distance between torso and barbell, violating *close*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Push knees out to create room between the legs.
- Imagine sitting upright and straight as you grab the bar.
- If hyperextended: flex abdominals until braced.
- If rounded: use blocks or hang position at heights where the natural curve can be maintained. Sumo deadlifts with weightlifting stance can also help.

---

### Grip

**Standard:** Hook grip — press the web between thumb and index finger deep against the barbell, wrap the thumb as far as possible, then wrap index and middle fingers over the thumb. This prevents slipping and lets the thumb apply upward force against the bar.

**Fault — Normal grip:** The thumb presses onto the fingers rather than hooking. Weaker than hook grip; creates fatigue and tension.

**Fault — False grip:** Even weaker; under no circumstances should it be used.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Loosen the thumb slightly if there is discomfort at the CMC or MCP joint.
- Use tape around the thumb to reduce discomfort.
- Only grip as tight as necessary without contracting forearms or biceps.
- Some athletes maintain hook grip throughout; others release during catch. Neither is definitively better.

---

### Wrist Position

**Standard:** Hook grip with relaxed wrists, aligned vertically with the arms. Relaxed wrists avoid overloading forearm flexors and help transfer vertical force.

**Fault — Flexed wrists:** Produces a horizontal force toward the body, can pull the torso and flex the spine, shifts balance forward, violates *close* and *stable*.

**Fault — Shins pulled back to compensate:** Violates *close* and shifts load from quads/glutes to hamstrings and lower back.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Once you grab the bar with hook grip, relax wrists and forearms.
- Do not force the wrist into any position.
- Lift the torso to remove slack from the arms.
- Imagine the arm and wrist as a taut rope attached at the shoulder.

---

### Chest Position

**Standard:** Push the chest out and contract it. This arches the thoracic spine slightly, contracts rear deltoids and lats, extends the shoulder toward anatomical position — all of which keep the barbell close. Contracting the chest keeps the humerus attached to the shoulder and internally rotated.

**Fault — Shoulder retraction + lat contraction:** Continues past the point of maximal stretch, creating excessive backward force and momentum.

**Fault — Upper back rounded:** Violates *close*, increases distance to barbell, stresses spine and back muscles.

**Coaching Tips:**
- If you have a logo on your shirt, point it directly away from you and expand it.
- For extra proprioception: have a partner pull your arm down slightly, then stand back to neutral and feel the chest engage. Have a partner touch the chest to reinforce the contraction.

---

### Elbow Position

**Standard:** Elbows point slightly behind, not directly to the sides. This is a natural result of pushing the chest out (which retracts the shoulder) and contracting the pectorals (which internally rotates the arm). Most coaches focus on chest position rather than elbow direction.

**Fault — Elbows rotated too far back:** Excessive lat contraction causes backward movement, limits upward extension and *close*.

**Fault — Elbows rotated forward (pointing to the sides):** Protracts shoulders, deflates chest, relaxes mid/upper back, creates a gap with the barbell.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Set up the lower body, grab the bar, and focus on pushing the chest out.
- Keep arms relaxed and stretched.
- Allow elbows to point in their natural position.
- You can place tape on the tip of the elbow and observe position on video.

---

### Arm & Shoulder Tension

**Standard:** Create tension in arms, shoulders, and upper traps by stretching the torso against the barbell — not by contracting those muscles directly. Slightly lengthening creates tension that increases contraction speed. Push the chest out and contract it against the barbell to keep the humerus in the shoulder joint.

**Fault — Arms flexed in start position:** Torso must bend more, overloading the lower back. If arms cannot hold, downward momentum is created, violating *fast*. Balance may shift forward.

**Fault — Shrugging:** Prematurely contracts traps and lateral deltoids, lowers the torso, can tilt shoulder blades forward and reduce upper back rigidity.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine sitting tall until you feel a stretch in arms and upper traps.
- Imagine keeping arms long and relaxed.
- Imagine arm and wrist as a taut rope attached at the shoulder.
- Use straps to help relax the arms while maintaining grip.

---

### Head Position

**Standard:** Lift the head approximately 45° from the floor — "the body follows the head." This makes it easier to push the chest out, extend the thoracic spine, and contract midback muscles and rear deltoids.

**Fault — Head too high (beyond 45°):** Maximizes thoracic extension but can introduce lumbar extension, remove abs tension, and shift balance backward.

**Fault — Head neutral or chin tucked:** Can hinder torso extension, relax spinal erectors, and cause the mid-back to round.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Focus on a point at 45° ahead of you.
- Place tape on throat and keep it visible from the front.
- Keep lower back straight as you lift your head.
- Arch through the thoracic spine; imagine pushing the chest logo forward.

## Deadlift Technique

### Liftoff

**Standard:** Lift shoulders and hips together, smoothly. Glutes contract to rotate knees outward; legs push while hips stay low; chest continues expanding; head remains up. Only lift as fast as these criteria can be maintained.

**Fault — Knees pushed back / excessive shoulder-over-bar focus:** Balance shifts backward, gap between legs and barbell, stress shifts to hamstrings and lower back.

**Fault — Hips, lower back, and shoulders aggressive without sufficient leg drive:** Shoulders move excessively backward, barbell can hit or swing around the knees.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Point chest straight ahead while pushing against the ground with legs.
- Shift balance forward until toes can grab the ground.
- Lift smoothly, not aggressively.
- Use a band across the sacrum and under the feet to provide feedback on hip vs. shoulder dominance.

---

### Transition

**Standard:** Allow the barbell to pass over the inside of the knee and VMO muscle. Keep feet flat, push off the ball of the foot, contract glutes to maintain knees externally rotated. The barbell will accelerate naturally as the body moves into a more mechanically advantageous position.

**Fault — Barbell passes over top of thighs:** Usually from a wide stance or unstable start position. Knees rotate inward, thighs block the bar, violating *close*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Push knees out and imagine keeping hips close to the barbell.
- Use a resistance band around knees at this position to train glute contraction.
- Perform deadlifts above the knee with blocks.
- Press all toes into the ground; use single-leg squats and lateral banded walks for foot stability.

---

### Above the Knee: Chest Position

**Standard:** Continue pushing the chest out actively as the barbell passes above the knee. This extends the shoulder, maintains thoracic extension, keeps the body close to the barbell, and maintains foot pressure on the ball of the foot (keeping the legs in a jumping position).

**Fault — Chest not actively maintained:** Creates a gap between barbell and thighs, increases barbell resistance, forces back/glutes/hamstrings to overwork. May result in backward or forward jump depending on strength.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Continue expanding the chest logo as the bar passes the knee.
- Imagine the position above the knee as preparation for a vertical jump.
- Perform block deadlifts at knee height, focusing on maintaining contact between thighs and bar through the movement.

---

### Above the Knee: Arm Bend

**Standard:** Keep arms stretched as the barbell passes the knee, similar to how arms swing back before a vertical jump. Maintaining a slight stretch lengthens and tensions the arm muscles, increasing their contraction speed during the extension.

**Fault — Arms bending:** Can compensate for improper balance, lack of postural strength, or abrupt speed change above the knee. Bending the arms lifts the barbell prematurely and reduces power of the extension.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Use straps and pause above the knee to build postural strength.
- Lifts from the hang; heavy lifts near the power position.
- Imagine the lift as a deadlift + snatch/clean from the power position.
- Place tape along the elbow crease; if arms bend, you will feel it fold.

---

### Above the Knee: Head Position

**Standard:** Continue lifting the head about 45° during the extension. This helps preserve balance, push the chest out, contract back muscles properly, and extend the arms to the correct contact point.

**Fault — Lowering the head:** Can result from leaning the torso back (overactive lower back), shifting knees under the bar, or stopping chest-out action above the knee. Creates a gap between torso and barbell, rounds the upper back, forces the body to lean back to generate range of motion.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Focus on a point 45° above the eye line throughout the lift.
- Place tape on the throat and keep it visible from the front.
- Place tape on the sternum and push it forward.
- Have a partner hold a PVC under the chin during the deadlift — do not rest on it.

---

### Contact Point

**Standard:** Reach the highest point possible while maintaining a jumping position. For the snatch: at the hip crease. For the clean: approximately 5–10 cm below the hip crease. These points allocate most work to the lower body.

**Fault — Contact below optimal points:** Usually caused by shifting balance backward or forward. Upper body must pull too early, limiting lower body extension. Violates *fast*.

> Note: Some athletes with very long arms have lower contact points due to the barbell shaft limiting snatch grip width. For these athletes, maintaining bent arms through the deadlift may be an acceptable trade-off to reach the optimal contact point.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Stand upright with a PVC or empty barbell using your snatch or clean grip — note where the barbell contacts the body. That is your contact point.

---

### Power Position

**Standard:** Arrive at the contact point in a natural jumping position — legs slightly bent, torso slightly flexed, chest out, balance forward on the ball of the foot. This maximizes transfer of vertical force in the shortest time.

**Fault — Legs over-extended with torso lagging:** Excessive lower back extension, backward momentum, limits vertical force.

**Fault — Torso extends early with knees still bent:** Two possible compensations — (1) shoulders go behind heels, knees move forward, heels lift; (2) early arm pull to compensate for barbell being far from contact point.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Stand up straight with a PVC/empty barbell in snatch or clean grip.
- Keep the PVC in contact with your body with straight arms, then bend forward as if about to jump vertically.

## Snatch

### Snatch Grip

**Standard:** Grip that places the barbell over the hip crease with straight arms. Wide enough for an optimal start position, full hip extension, and a stable overhead squat.

**Fault — Too wide (barbell above hip crease):** Contact point at the abdomen — not powerful. Increases arm tension, can cause arm bend or forearm tension, violates *timing* and *fast*.

**Fault — Too narrow:** Lowers the contact point, lengthens the distance to lift the barbell overhead, forces upper body to do more work than the legs and hips.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Place the barbell over the hip crease, then slide hands outward until arms are straight.
- Stand tall, chest out, shoulders relaxed.
- Use a hook grip.

---

### Extension: Lower Body Coordination

**Standard:** Use ankles, knees, hips, elbows, and shoulders to extend the whole body. When the barbell reaches the hip crease, legs push through the ground to extend knees and hips simultaneously while maintaining ball-of-foot pressure. Immediately after, ankles plantarflex.

**Fault — Insufficient ankle plantarflexion:** Incomplete extension; reduces vertical force intensity.

**Fault — Upper body rushes before leg/hip extension:** Legs cannot push powerfully; upper body must compensate.

**Fault — Knees extend without hips:** Incomplete hip extension; can cause sliding backward. Also increases distance between barbell and body.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Keep balance on the ball of the foot; use tape on the floor to practice staying on the line.
- Imagine the extension as a vertical jump without leaving the ground.
- Pause at the top of the pull to test balance.
- Standing explosive calf raises, kettlebell high pulls, leg press at 45° or 90°.

---

### Extension: Upper Body Coordination

**Standard:** Elevate elbows and shoulders as the ankles plantarflex. Upper trapezius, lateral deltoids, and biceps lift the barbell after the legs extend. This should feel like one simultaneous motion. Abs and back muscles hold the torso rigid. Forearms stay relaxed.

**Fault — Torso hyperextension:** Shifts balance backward, limits plantarflexion and leg extension, increases distance to barbell, can cause forward or backward jump.

**Fault — Upper body not used after leg extension:** Arms produce downward force on barbell's upward momentum; barbell arcs away from the body. Athlete must jump forward.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Perform snatch pulls from the hip crease.
- Have a partner place a stick vertically at the end of the barbell — pull vertically to avoid hitting it.
- Pause at the power position to ensure extension starts from the correct position.

---

### Extension: Head & Chest Position

**Standard:** Push chest out and maintain head up about 45° during the extension. This preserves thoracic extension and contracts rear deltoids and back muscles to prevent the barbell from drifting away.

**Fault — Head drifts forward/downward:** Flexes thoracic spine, protracts shoulders, moves torso away from barbell. Can result in the barbell hitting the forehead. Softens torso and reduces force transfer.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Keep head up and chest out — imagine keeping your head above water.
- Place tape on throat and keep it visible from the front.
- Place tape on the hip and diaphragm — focus on lengthening the distance between them while pointing straight ahead.
- Pause at top of pull to evaluate gap with barbell.
- Perform snatch-grip upright rows to the chest focused on head position, then add explosive calf raise.

---

### Extension: Height for Snatch Pull

**Standard:** Use upper and lower body together to lift the barbell to the bottom of the chest. This is the maximum the athlete can pull vertically while maintaining position, and the minimum height to catch the most weight in the lowest overhead squat.

**Fault — Pulling too low:** Reduces potential force from traps and lateral deltoids. Insufficient time for athlete to catch in a low position. Stressful on joints from barbell's downward momentum without sufficient upward momentum.

**Fault — Pulling too high:** Usually from balance issues, lack of coordination, or waiting too long to drop. Reduces time available to catch in a low overhead squat. Athlete often catches in a quarter or half squat.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Go to a squat rack, stand straight, plantarflex, measure chest height. Set a stick/PVC at that height. Perform snatch pull — the end of the barbell should only lightly touch the PVC.
- Have a partner hold a PVC at the correct height to aim for.
- Keep wrists relaxed; use straps if necessary.

---

### Extension: Elbow Position

**Standard:** Lift elbows vertically and in line with the torso. This keeps the barbell close to the body, minimizes arc, and maximizes height. Maintain chest out throughout.

**Fault — Elbows rotated backward:** Restricts internal rotation and shoulder elevation. Caused by tensing forearms, hyperextending torso, retracted posture, or limited shoulder internal rotation. Shifts stress from upper trapezius/lateral deltoids to weaker forearm extensors and wrists. Barbell may collide with ribs or chest.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Stand straight, forearms relaxed, and point elbows toward the ceiling.
- Use straps to relax forearms.
- Face a mirror sideways and perform snatch pull with a pause at the chest.
- Perform slow-motion snatch.
- Perform snatch-grip upright rows for isolated upper body coordination.

---

### Pulling Under the Barbell

**Standard:** Actively dorsiflex the ankles to slide the feet downward and laterally after the extension. As the slide occurs, arms/shoulders/upper traps continue elevating the barbell. Once the bar passes the chest, the wrist pulls onto it and the triceps extend the arm. Sliding is the quickest way to bring heels down from plantarflexion and allow hips and knees to assist in pulling under.

**Fault — Upper body stops pulling after extension:** Reduces upward force and height; barbell changes direction sooner with more downward momentum during catch. Excess stress on elbows, shoulders, hips, knees.

**Fault — Feet adjusted laterally during upward phase (jumping):** Lengthens time in air where no force can be applied. Must wait to land before pulling or squatting. Violates *timing*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- From start position, imagine jumping into the catch position.
- Finish slide and pull simultaneously.
- Imagine bringing heels down as if creating a splash in a puddle.
- Set a stick/PVC at chest height in a squat rack. Perform a snatch speed pull (panda pull) — barbell should only lightly contact the PVC and reach the clavicle.

---

### Catch: Internal Rotation Overhead

**Standard:** Maintain internal rotation force as the barbell passes overhead. After bar reaches chest height, continue pulling straight up rather than backward. The shoulder externally rotates, but exerting an internal rotation force stops backward momentum and aligns the bar directly over the midfoot. Internal rotation creates a strong base, allows back muscles to absorb stress, and introduces torque in the elbow and wrist.

**Fault — Allowing arms to follow barbell's momentum:** Barbell ends up behind the body (sometimes behind the heel). Violates *close*. Excessive stress on shoulders and biceps.

**Fault — Looking upward during catch:** Makes it difficult to place the bar behind the head; prevents shoulder retraction and back muscle contraction; loads shoulders instead of back; arms cannot internally rotate, so elbows align with the barbell's force line and bend under heavy loads.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Point elbows behind you and squeeze shoulder blades as if holding a vertical pen with your mid-back.
- Tuck chin after the barbell passes the face.
- Imagine twisting your arm forward like a key in a lock.
- Strengthen with dumbbell or PVC held overhead — practice rotating the whole arm clockwise and counterclockwise.

---

### Catch: Wrist Position

**Standard:** Extend the wrist and supinate the forearm while maintaining internal elbow rotation. This creates external torque in the wrist that counters internal elbow torque — locking the wrist so the barbell rests on the base of the wrist, a stable shelf to push from.

**Fault — Wrist extended without torque:** Barbell sits in the palm rather than the base of the wrist. Greater stress; limits supportable weight. Violates *close*.

**Fault — Neutral wrist position:** Unstable — can move forward or backward easily. Can occur from tense forearms preventing quick wrist extension.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Lift arm overhead and turn the hand so the thumb points toward you. From there, turn away from the body so the base of the thumbs faces the ceiling.
- Imagine pushing the wrists toward the ceiling.
- Alternatively, imagine pushing two walls away from you.

---

### Catch: Power Snatch

**Standard:** Flex knees and hips simultaneously with barbell balanced over the midfoot. Wrists, elbows, and shoulders align over the midfoot, maximizing support.

**Fault — Knees bend without sufficient hip flexion:** Torso stays vertical to prevent barbell going further backward. Only compensation is knee flexion, causing forward running during recovery.

**Fault — Hips bend without sufficient knee flexion:** Usually from barbell not reaching enough height. Some athletes compensate by pushing the humerus forward — shoulder is in a vulnerable position.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Snatch pull + power snatch combo to build pull consistency.
- Pause during catch to adjust to a more upright position.
- Snatch presses behind the neck at quarter squat height; partial overhead squats; drop snatch to quarter squat height.

---

### Catch: Head Position

**Standard:** Lower the head to about 45° when supporting the barbell overhead. This brings the head slightly forward, moves the barbell backward, and makes it easier to align the barbell over the midfoot. It also helps retract the back muscles to support the weight.

**Fault — Looking up:** Shifts balance backward, can rotate shoulder blade downward, limits arm elevation, places the barbell in front and load on the shoulders.

**Fault — Pushing head forward:** Violates *close* and *stable* — can shift balance excessively forward, flex thoracic and lumbar spine, and create shoulder impingement.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Place tape on the ground about 1m away. Finish the catch and look at the tape simultaneously.
- Practice snatch presses behind the neck, overhead squats, and drop snatch at various heights with head down at 45°.

---

### Catch: Overhead Squat Balance

**Standard:** Align wrists, elbows, and shoulders with hips as close to ankles as possible. This maximizes barbell support, places load on quads/glutes/midback, and aligns the barbell over the midfoot.

**Fault — Balance too far forward (ball of foot/toes):** Knees travel forward, heels may rise. Athletes often lean back to compensate, reducing the base and violating *stable*.

**Fault — Balance too far backward (heels):** Hips push back without knees going forward. Toes may lift. Leaning forward to compensate increases lower back stress and misaligns the shoulder with the barbell.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Overhead squat variations to the depth where balance begins to fail: to a box, with pauses, assisted by a band looped around a squat rack.
- Snatch grip presses and drop snatches to depth limits.

---

### Catch: Lower Back Tension

**Standard:** Maintain the most upright posture while balancing the barbell over the midfoot. Minimizes backward momentum, shifts stress from lower back to legs and hips, and allows more effective force transfer through the torso.

**Fault — Lower back rounds:** Can result from improper pull or catch (backward momentum shifts balance to heels); ankle dorsiflexion restrictions; narrow squat stance (thighs block torso); insufficient abdominal tension.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Snatch pulls with and without pause at the chest.
- Strengthen anterior shin muscles: elevated split squats, narrow stance squats, step-ups to high box.
- Overhead squat variations to the depth where lower back begins to round: partial squats, box squats, squats with hanging weights, behind-the-neck snatch press at that depth.

---

### Catch: Overhead Squat Width

**Standard:** Catch using the same stance as a back squat (~shoulder-width). This is the strongest position for leg support and allows the lowest catch, reducing the need to pull the barbell excessively high.

**Fault — Stance too narrow (deadlift stance maintained):** Thighs block the torso during squat; athletes compensate by catching higher.

**Fault — Stance too wide:** Can result from lack of strength/mobility for a deep squat, or from pulling the barbell too high (insufficient time to drop under it).

**Coaching Tips:**
- Power snatch + overhead squat to reinforce squat stance.
- Place mats on the lateral edge of your feet at squat width, and stay within those lines during the snatch.
- Snatch turnovers to coordinate pull-under and footwork.

---

### Catch: Knee Alignment

**Standard:** Turn feet outward and align with knees to push through the midline of the foot. Exact angle depends on hip structure and ankle mobility.

**Fault — Knees too far out past lateral foot edge:** Reduces adductor contribution, makes recovery harder.

**Fault — Knees rotating inward:** Can block the torso, push hips back, cause falling backward or forward barbell movement, and allow the spine to round.

**Coaching Tips:**
- 3-level snatch (quarter, half, full) to build positional strength.
- Pause during power snatch catch, then perform overhead squat to depth where knee position remains solid.
- Balance on one foot, focus on even pressure on first toe, fifth toe, and heel, then perform overhead squats pausing at the depth where foot balance changes.

---

### Catch: Arm Bend

**Standard:** Straighten arms completely during the catch. In competition, bent arms are only permitted if the athlete is physically unable to extend the elbow. Full arm extension allows internal rotation and back muscle recruitment, dispersing force over a larger area.

**Fault — Bent arms during catch:** Poor timing (pulling too high delays the catch, so the body and barbell drop together); tense forearms delay arm movement during the catch.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Snatch pulls with and without pause to improve extension accuracy; use straps.
- Focus on maintaining internal rotation as arms move overhead.
- Snatch-grip upright rows with straps; add muscle snatch while standing; add plantarflexion during concentric and dorsiflexion during eccentric/catch; finally combine with deadlift and squat.

---

### Catch: Symmetry

**Standard:** Catch the barbell evenly and parallel with the floor to minimize side-to-side movement and allow a straight recovery.

**Fault — Uneven catch:** Can result from asymmetrical strength or positioning in the torso during extension, or from one side rotating or pressing overhead faster than the other.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Snatch-grip upright rows in front of a mirror.
- Snatch pulls with pause at the chest in a mirror to coordinate right and left sides.
- For uneven catch: muscle snatch, overhead squat, drop snatch, snatch turnover — all in front of a mirror.

---

### Catch: Rotation

**Standard:** Catch the barbell evenly to minimize rotational movement. Keeps the barbell aligned over the midfoot and disperses load evenly on both sides.

**Fault — Overhead rotation:** Usually caused by a stronger side that pulls, rotates, and extends faster. Stability, flexibility, and mobility asymmetries also contribute.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Have a partner rotate the barbell into a symmetrical position after the catch; memorize and replicate it.
- Incorporate snatch turnovers to ensure vertical pull into a squat.
- Snatch deadlift in a mirror with pauses to ensure symmetrical pushing.
- Snatch-grip upright row + muscle snatch from the hip to train symmetrical transition.

---

### Snatch Recovery

**Standard:** Lift the chest aggressively and contract the glutes to keep the barbell moving vertically over the midfoot as the athlete stands up. Hips and shoulders rise together. Head maintains its 45° downward tilt.

**Fault — Torso inclines forward:** Usually caused by backward barbell swing, insufficient internal rotation overhead, or excessive forward head during catch. Athletes compensate by raising hips, requiring greater shoulder flexion and introducing instability.

**Fault — Hips move backward during recovery:** Can result from pulling knees and thighs inward while extending the hip, or rushing the recovery.

**Coaching Tips:**
- After catching, have a partner place a stick vertically aligned with the midfoot. Maintain barbell contact with the stick while standing.
- Pause squats, squats with bands around the knees, lateral squats, lateral step-ups — all develop glute-adductor coordination.
- Place tape on the side of the waist; focus on lifting it vertically during recovery.

---

## Clean

### Clean Grip

**Standard:** At least one fist-width outside the shoulder. Wide enough for an optimal start position, high contact point, and stable rack position. For most athletes, this width is also manageable for catching in a front squat.

**Fault — Too wide (approaching snatch grip):** Increases arm tension, may cause arm bend or forearm tension, more torso flexion needed to grab the bar.

**Fault — Too narrow (natural resting position):** Appears to minimize lower back stress due to more upright torso, but the contact point is lower, requiring more upper body work during extension.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Measure one fist-width outside the shoulder and grip outside of that distance.
- Use your hand to measure — body proportions vary.
- Use a hook grip.
- Wide-grip cleans are an assistance exercise for intermediate lifters needing help staying upright in the catch.

---

### Knee & Arm Alignment

**Standard:** Feet turned outward, glutes used to align knees with the midline of the foot, arms kept straight. Legs can rub against but not push the elbows. Brings hips closer to barbell, keeps torso upright, preserves arm tension.

**Fault — Excessive outward knee rotation:** Shifts pressure to lateral foot edge, reduces adductor contribution, pushes knees against elbows bending them, forces torso to flex, shifts balance forward. Also creates slack in the arms that abruptly changes speed and position.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Only push knees out until they align with the midline of the foot.
- If knees still push against arms in a hip-width stance, widen the grip slightly.

---

### Extension: Lower Body Coordination (Clean)

**Standard:** Identical to snatch — use ankles, knees, hips to extend maximally. Contact point is 5–10 cm below the hip crease for the clean. Since contact occurs earlier than in the snatch, the clean extension relies more on lower body strength and power.

**Fault — Insufficient ankle flexion or duration:** Arms tense, reduces vertical force transfer, can cause over-pulling.

**Fault — Upper body rushes before leg/hip extension:** Legs cannot push powerfully; upper body must compensate.

**Fault — Knees extend without hips:** Reduces vertical force; can cause sliding backward.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Same as snatch extension: ball-of-foot tape drill, imagine vertical jump without leaving the ground.
- Explosive standing calf raises; leg press at 45° or 90°.

---

### Extension: Upper Body Coordination (Clean)

**Standard:** Elevate elbows and shoulders as ankles plantarflex. Upper trapezius, lateral deltoids, and biceps lift the barbell after the legs extend, feeling like one simultaneous motion. Abs and back hold the torso rigid. Forearms stay relaxed.

**Fault — Torso hyperextension:** Same consequences as in the snatch — shifts balance backward, limits plantarflexion, increases lower back and shoulder joint stress.

**Fault — Upper body not used after leg extension:** Beginners who focus solely on hip drive; intermediate athletes who tense arms and shoulders. Barbell arcs away from the body, athlete must jump forward.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Clean pulls from the contact point.
- Partner places a stick vertically at the end of the barbell — pull vertically to avoid hitting it.
- Pause at the power position.
- Imagine pulling up your pants by the seams to your belly button.

---

### Extension: Head & Chest Position (Clean)

**Standard:** Push chest out and maintain head up about 45° during the extension. This supports thoracic extension, contracts rear deltoids and back muscles, and prevents barbell from drifting.

**Fault — Head drifting forward or downward:** Flexes thoracic spine, protracts shoulders, moves torso away from barbell. Gap increases resistance and reduces vertical force. Barbell may collide with clavicle or throat during catch.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Head up, chest out — imagine keeping head above water.
- Place tape on throat; keep it visible from the front.
- Place tape on diaphragm; imagine pushing it forward with relaxed arms and shoulders.
- Use a mirror and pause at top of pull.

---

### Extension: Height for Clean Pull

**Standard:** Extend legs and pull with upper body in one fluid motion to the navel. This is the minimum height to catch the most weight in the lowest front squat, and it maximizes the force from legs and pull speed from the upper body.

**Fault — Pulling too low:** Barbell too heavy or rushing under. Most athletes lack mobility/time to catch from a low pull. Risk of wrist sprain or barbell dropping on thighs. Violates *timing* and *fast*.

**Fault — Pulling too high:** Balance problems in deadlift, poor coordination, or waiting too long to drop. Reduces time to reach a low front squat. Athletes often catch in a quarter or half squat. Violates *timing* and *low*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Keep wrists relaxed; use straps if necessary.
- Perform clean pulls in front of a mirror.
- Set a stick/PVC at navel height in a squat rack — the barbell end should only lightly contact it.
- Have a partner hold a PVC at the correct pull height.

---

### Extension: Elbow Position (Clean)

**Standard:** Use upper trapezius, lateral deltoids, and biceps to point the elbow vertically. This keeps the barbell close, minimizes arc, and maximizes height. Maintain chest out.

**Fault — Elbows rotated backward:** Restricts internal rotation and shoulder elevation. Shifts load to brachioradialis, changes pull into a reverse curl, increases distance from the body, and creates a greater arc. Barbell can crash onto shoulders, clavicle, or neck.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Stand straight, point elbows toward the ceiling; use straps if needed.
- Imagine lifting your shirt to expose your navel.
- Stand sideways in front of a mirror; perform clean pull with pause to observe elbow position.
- Clean-grip upright rows to focus on upper body coordination.

---

### Pulling Under the Barbell (Clean)

**Standard:** Actively dorsiflex ankles to slide feet downward and laterally after the extension. Arms, shoulders, and upper traps continue elevating the barbell to the shoulders. Once the barbell passes the navel, the wrist begins to pull onto it and the arms rotate backward then forward. Sliding is the quickest way to get the heels down and allow hips and knees to assist in pulling under.

**Fault — Pulling too high before dropping:** Beginners who lack confidence. Creates timing violation — body and barbell drop together, absorbing impact through elbows, shoulders, hips, knees.

**Fault — Jumping (feet adjusted during upward phase):** Lengthens time in air; must wait to land before squatting. Violates *timing*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine jumping into the catch position from the start position.
- Imagine lowering heels as if creating a splash in a puddle.
- Finish slide and pull simultaneously.
- Set stick/PVC at navel height in a squat rack — barbell should lightly contact it during a clean panda pull.

---

### Catch: Front Squat Grip

**Standard:** Maintain a full grip through the catch to maximize connection with the barbell. This keeps athletes pulling and rotating the barbell to stay close and move it faster during transition. Athletes who physically cannot hold with a full grip should maintain it at least until the elbows begin rotating upward.

**Fault — Releasing grip before elbows rotate under barbell:** Can result from clean pull errors forcing position adjustment, or from limited shoulder mobility. Disconnects from barbell, slows catch, places athlete in a weak position, barbell may crash or roll off.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Wrap straps around the bar to help maintain full grip.
- Load a barbell in a squat rack at slightly lower than squat height. Slowly press against it, pausing at tight or asymmetrical spots.
- Progress with jerk dips, quarter/pause/box front squats.
- Perform clean pulls + cleans to ensure consistent pulling mechanics.

---

### Catch: Power Clean

**Standard:** Flex knees and hips simultaneously with barbell balanced over the midfoot. Shoulders, hips, and ankles align as closely as possible, maximizing support.

**Fault — Knees flex without sufficient hip flexion:** Usually from excessive backward lean during deadlift or extension. Torso stays upright to prevent barbell going further backward; only compensation is knee flexion. Places stress on knees and lower back.

**Fault — Hips flex without sufficient knee flexion:** False sense of getting under the barbell. Some athletes cannot flex knees forward due to stiff ankles. Excessive hip flexion causes the arm to support the barbell diagonally — shoulder impingement risk.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Clean pull + power clean to build pull muscle memory.
- Pause at navel during clean pull if necessary.
- Power clean without split to focus on vertical movement up and down; then combine power clean without split + power clean with split.
- Pause during catch to assess balance.

---

### Catch: Head Position (Clean)

**Standard:** Keep head up about 45° throughout the entire clean, including the catch. Easier to push the chest out and hold the back rigid; greater core stability. Maintains barbell over the AC joint, clavicle, and midfoot.

**Fault — Chin tucked or looking down:** Flexes torso, lowers elbow angle, relaxes back muscles, rounds the spine, shifts squat balance forward. Places pressure on wrists and shoulders. Elbows may contact knees, compressing forearm and risking wrist sprain.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Focus on a spot at 45° throughout the entire clean; imagine keeping head above water; place tape on throat.
- Maintain chest out as you squat; place tape on center of chest pointing forward.
- Sit up tall in the squat; use tape on waist and chest to keep distance constant.
- Clean pulls + cleans to reinforce head and chest position.

---

### Catch: Front Squat Balance

**Standard:** Shoulders aligned over the midfoot with hips and ankles as close as possible. Minimizes lower back stress, enhances stability, maximizes ground connection.

**Fault — Too far forward (knees pushed excessively):** Torso cannot lean back to compensate in a full clean. Heels may lift; balance shifts forward. Athletes may end up running forward.

**Fault — Too far backward (limited torso mobility):** Rounding the back to push barbell forward is a compensation that stresses back muscles and spine. Narrow stance, excessive backward lean, or ankle restrictions may cause this.

**Coaching Tips:**
- 3-level cleans (quarter, half, full) to build a consistent catch position.
- No-hands front squat to balance the barbell.
- Lunges with front foot elevated; hold kettlebell between legs and sit down (not back).

---

### Catch: Lower Back Tension (Clean)

**Standard:** Maintain the most upright posture while balancing the barbell over the midfoot. Keeps the lower back rigid, transfers force more effectively, shifts stress to legs and hips.

**Fault — Lower back rounds:** Improper pull or rack position (backward momentum shifts balance to heels); athletes may round the back to push the barbell forward, risking a technical foul if elbows contact knees. Narrow squat stance or ankle restrictions can also cause this.

**Coaching Tips:**
- See "Catch: Elbow Position" section for elbow solutions.
- See "Catch: Front Squat Width" and "Clean Recovery" for squat suggestions.
- Perform squats holding a 5–10 kg disc in front to assist in maintaining upright torso.

---

### Catch: Front Squat Width

**Standard:** Catch using the same stance as a front squat (~shoulder-width). Strongest position for leg strength; allows the lowest squat; catches in this position reduce the necessary extension height and downward momentum.

**Fault — Stance too narrow (deadlift stance maintained):** Thighs block the torso; athletes compensate by catching higher, requiring higher extension — violates *low*.

**Fault — Stance too wide:** Can result from inability to perform a deep squat; or from pulling too high (insufficient time to drop under). Leaning forward with a wide stance may cause the barbell to roll off the shoulders.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Paused clean pull + clean to coordinate pull-under and footwork.
- If different stances feel more natural, consider training the front squat in the same stance as the clean.
- Place mats on the lateral edge of feet at front squat width — stay within those lines during the clean.

---

### Catch: Knee Alignment (Clean)

**Standard:** Feet turned outward, aligned with knees. Must push through the midline of the feet. Exact angle depends on hip structure and ankle mobility.

**Fault — Knees rotating inward:** Wide stance can cause this. Also from losing hip tension at the bottom of the front squat.

**Fault — Knees too far out past lateral foot edge:** Often to create space for the torso or to compensate for a narrow stance. Reduces lateral base and recovery power.

**Coaching Tips:**
- 3-level clean (quarter, half, full) to build positional strength.
- Balance on one foot with even pressure on first toe, fifth toe, and heel. Perform no-hands front squats, pause front squats, step-ups to high box.

---

### Catch: Elbow Position

**Standard:** Barbell across the collarbone, contacting the clavicle and AC joint of each shoulder. Shoulder blades protract slightly to push shoulders forward and create a shelf. Chest must be pushed out and elbows moved outward to engage midback muscles strongly. Shoulders elevate slightly; elbows elevate only as high as necessary to maintain the shelf.

**Fault — Elbows pushed inward and excessively upward:** Can lift the barbell off the clavicle and press it against the throat — risk of impeding blood flow and fainting (carotid artery). Or excessive shoulder protraction flexes midback, pushes torso backward, increases distance from barbell and violates *close*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- During transition, focus on keeping the shirt logo visible to an imaginary audience in front of you.
- During front squats, place tape on each medial epicondyle and keep them as far apart as possible.
- Strengthen elbow rotation with wide-grip biceps curls.
- Lift barbell overhead and lower slowly to rack position (reverse curl to shoulders); pause where tightness or asymmetry appears.

---

### Catch: Elbow Symmetry

**Standard:** Maintain symmetrical elbow position during the catch. Equal loading on each side reduces injury risk and minimizes rotational movement during recovery.

**Fault — Asymmetrical pull:** One elbow higher or one elbow pointing back. Can result from movement restrictions or strength imbalances during the clean pull.

**Fault — Symmetrical pull but asymmetrical catch:** Movement restrictions in elbow flexion, shoulder flexion, shoulder protraction, or external rotation.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Clean-grip upright rows to coordinate upper trapezius, lateral deltoid, and biceps between right and left sides.
- Progress to clean pulls with and without pause from various heights.
- Single-arm kettlebell front squat on restricted side with deep breaths.

---

### Clean Recovery

**Standard:** Maintain head up at 45°, lift the chest aggressively, and contract the glutes forcefully to move the barbell vertically over the midfoot. Hips and shoulders rise together to prevent torso forward movement or elbow angle change.

**Fault — Hips rise faster than shoulders:** Caused by catching the barbell forward or backward (improper pull/transition), looking down during catch, or upper/lower body movement restrictions. Places excessive stress on wrists and shoulders; forces lower back and hamstrings to overwork.

**Fault — Hips rise fast even with proper catch:** Difficulty in hip extension causes knees to straighten quickly, hips shift upward and backward. Shifts workload to knees and lower back.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Pause front squats; 1¼ front squats; front squats with bands around knees; no-hands front squats; step-ups to high box.
- Place tape on the side of the waist; focus on lifting it vertically during recovery.

---

## Jerk

### Adjustment after the Clean

**Standard:** Adjust grip outward as you recover from the clean, using the upward momentum from the legs during the recovery to push the barbell off the shoulders. A wider grip allows more chest-out, contracts back muscles more easily, shortens the distance the barbell must travel overhead, and provides greater control overhead.

**Fault — Adjusting after standing:** Wastes time and energy; introduces instability while the back and legs are still under tension.

**Fault — Grip too wide (approaching snatch grip):** Most athletes lack the mobility for this.

**Fault — Maintaining clean grip:** Lengthens time required to place barbell overhead; violates *timing* and *stable*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Arch slightly through the thoracic spine — expand the chest logo or stretch tape across the sternum.
- Find grip width by holding a band with a supine grip as if completing a chin-up, hands under chin; pull the band as far apart as possible while keeping the medial elbows pointing forward.

---

### Jerk Stance: Width

**Standard:** Step one leg inward on each side to return to a hip-width stance — identical to the snatch and clean start position. Feet flat, rotated outward at the same angle. Using the same stance across all three lifts maximizes vertical power and training variety.

**Fault — Maintaining front squat/catch stance (too wide for most):** Shortens extension height; horizontal leg force reduces vertical force; violates *fast* and *timing*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- After the clean, position yourself to jump vertically.
- Use tape parallel to the outer sides of the feet from the start position; step back until the outer sides of your feet are inside the tape.

---

### Jerk Stance: Foot Balance

**Standard:** Balance over the ankle bone. Forms a vertical line with the barbell on the shoulders — most efficient path for the barbell and maximizes stability in an upright posture.

**Fault — Balance toward heels:** Gives a false sense of balance; difficult to lift the heels during extension, reducing vertical force.

**Fault — Toes lifted to keep balance on heels:** Reduces base size and plantarflexion range, violating both *stable* and *fast*.

**Fault — Balance over midfoot or ball of foot:** Forces forward lean; violates *close* and *stable*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Have a partner place a stick vertically on the outer edge of the foot, aligned with the ankle joint. If the stick is in front of the barbell, shift forward; if behind, shift back.
- Brace core as if absorbing a punch.

---

### Jerk Stance: Leg Position

**Standard:** Stand with straight legs after the clean. Locking knees and hips shifts support from muscles to bones, giving legs a moment to recover from the clean and prepare for the jerk. Straight legs also naturally position the barbell over the ankle bone.

**Fault — Knees remain flexed:** Fatigues quadriceps, reducing power; can aim the barbell forward, pulling balance forward. Compensating with a lower back arch places excessive stress on the lumbar spine.

**Fault — Knees extended but hips not:** Forces torso to lean forward, violating *close*. Athletes may anteriorly rotate the pelvis and hyperextend the lower back, increasing injury risk over time.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Stand up straight as if "at attention."
- Contract glutes and extend knees.
- Have a partner tap knees and glutes with a PVC as reminders.
- Incorporate reverse planks to strengthen the ability to tighten the core while extending the legs.

---

### Rack Position: Head Position

**Standard:** Lift head to about 45° from the floor. Creates a slight backward shift in balance that makes it easier to push the chest out, extend elbows outward, extend the thoracic spine, and place the barbell in a wide and stable position on the deltoids and clavicle.

**Fault — Looking down or neutral head:** Lowers elbows, moves barbell forward, tensions the arms, flexes the thoracic spine, reduces vertical force transfer.

**Fault — Excessive backward neck extension:** Shifts balance to heels, reduces base, limits heel lift during extension; forces rest of the torso to arch excessively, reducing torso stability.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Keep eyes fixed on a spot 45° above eye line; place tape on throat.
- Push chest out; place tape on sternum and push it forward.
- Brace core as if absorbing a punch.

---

### Rack Position: Elbow Angle

**Standard:** Lower elbows during adjustment after the clean. Forearms more vertical — advantageous for transferring vertical force. Reduces shoulder protraction. Shifts support toward the spinal erectors. Minimum: elbows in front of the barbell when viewed from the side.

**Fault — Elbows raised high without grip adjustment:** Creates a triceps-extension-type position — weaker motion that can shift barbell forward. Athletes with relatively longer forearms cannot do this without flexing the thoracic spine, violating *close*.

**Fault — Elbows aligned with or behind barbell:** Cannot create a shelf to support the barbell; must hold it with the arms and extend the neck. Stresses wrists, arms, shoulders, and spine.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine resting a tray of food on your palm and shoulder; keep the tray parallel to the floor.
- Brace core as if absorbing a punch.

---

### Rack Position: Shoulders

**Standard:** Barbell rests on the clavicle and AC joint of each shoulder. Shoulder blades protract slightly; shoulder elevates slightly and externally rotates to create a shelf — less pronounced than in the clean because the torso is more upright. Midback and spinal erectors do most of the supporting; push the chest out strongly to engage midback.

**Fault — Tensing shoulders (barbell lifted off collarbone):** Barbell presses toward the windpipe or superior carotid triangle. Risk of obstructed breathing or fainting (carotid artery compression). Anterior deltoids must support the barbell — not strong enough for heavy loads; easy to lose position during the jerk dip.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Strengthen midback with barbell/dumbbell rows, reverse crossovers, and reverse flyes.
- Incorporate no-hands front squats to get comfortable supporting the bar on the clavicles.
- Place tape on medial epicondyles and keep them as far apart as possible while pushing the chest out.

---

### Rack Position: Elbow Symmetry

**Standard:** Maintain symmetrical elbow position in the rack and through the jerk dip. Ensures equal loading on both sides, reduces injury risk, and minimizes extra movements that could disrupt extension and catch.

**Fault — One elbow higher:** Can result from inadequate shoulder flexion. Changes the path each arm travels to straighten, causing an uneven catch.

**Fault — Asymmetrical shoulder position (gap between shoulder and barbell):** Excessive shoulder extension on one side, difficulty protracting, or inadequate shoulder external rotation. Barbell may slip during jerk dip.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Lift barbell overhead and lower slowly to rack position — pause at tightness or asymmetry.
- Jerk dip variations in a mirror: static holds at bottom, paused at bottom, touch-and-go.

---

### Rack Position: Jerk Grip

**Standard:** Grab the barbell with as full a grip as possible while maintaining a strong shelf. Barbell as close as possible to the base of the palm to align with the forearm bones. Hook grip is maintained by some; others release the thumb — this is individual and should occur naturally.

**Fault — Full grip but forearms/hands tense:** Can push barbell forward or off the shoulders. Slows vertical force transfer.

**Fault — False grip (barbell in palm without thumb):** Compensates for elbow or shoulder mobility restrictions. Can be dangerous overhead as barbell can slip.

**Fault — Less than two fingers gripping:** Hands twist inward, wrists lift laterally — reduces vertical force and must correct before lockout.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Use straps with a full overhand grip at rack width, push overhead, then lower slowly to rack position as a reverse curl — pause at tightness or asymmetry.
- Load a barbell in a squat rack at slightly lower than front squat height; slowly press against it, pausing at tight or asymmetrical spots. Use straps to help maintain full grip.

---

### Jerk Dip: Depth

**Standard:** For most athletes, the depth where the knees align directly over the toes. This is the optimal distance to generate considerable momentum while maintaining barbell vertically over the ankle bone and providing enough time to stop sharply and generate elastic energy.

**Fault — Short dip with hip bend:** Body moves away from barbell, violating *close* and *stable*. Barbell shifts from ankle bone to midfoot, creating a diagonal path. Legs not sufficiently loaded for a powerful extension.

**Fault — Knee-only dip:** Knees must pass the toes for enough depth, shifting balance forward. Overloads small muscles (ankle dorsiflexors, lower back).

**Coaching Tips:**
- Have a partner hold a stick from ankle bone to barbell; dip so barbell stays on this line.
- Have partner hold a stick vertically over the midline of the foot; dip only until the middle of the patella touches it.
- Perform jerk dips and jerks with the stick to develop muscle memory and rhythm.
- Imagine holding a glass of water in the palm — dip without spilling.

---

### Jerk Dip: Knee Angle

**Standard:** Maintain balance over the ankle bone and use glutes to rotate knees out. This prepares the body to push forcefully and quickly against the barbell by pushing from the entire foot, aligning foot/shin/thigh, and loading the glutes.

**Fault — Knees caving inward:** Weak glutes/hamstrings, tight adductors, dominant quadriceps, or ankle mobility restrictions. Does not load glutes for a powerful hip extension.

**Fault — Knees too far out:** Reduces adductor contribution to hip extension; shifts foot pressure to lateral edge; can overstress posterior and lateral knee tissues.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Have a partner hold a stick vertically over the midline of the foot; dip until the middle of the patella touches it.
- Alternatively, face a wall with toes touching; dip until the patella touches the wall.
- Pause after the eccentric portion of the jerk dip to feel balance and knee alignment.
- Paused jerk + jerk — ensure identical balance and posture in each jerk dip.

---

### Jerk Dip: Head & Elbow Position

**Standard:** Maintain head at 45° from the floor. Elbows keep the same angle established after adjusting from the clean and remain rotated outward. These actions prevent the torso from collapsing by loading the lats and midback rather than the arms and shoulders.

**Fault — Head drops:** Can pull the torso forward, flex the thoracic spine, move the barbell toward the midfoot. Violates *close*, *stable*, and *fast* (reduces ability to change direction quickly and transfer force). Loads wrists and forearms.

**Fault — Elbows lower during dip:** Engages arms too early; loads wrists and forearms; reduces force transfer overhead.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Focus on a point 45° in front; place tape on throat.
- Imagine keeping head above water as you dip.
- Perform jerk dips in a mirror to ensure elbows remain stationary; add a pause before standing.

---

### Jerk Dip: Speed & Timing

**Standard:** Reach optimal depth smoothly then change direction immediately and forcefully. A smooth but fast dip increases the barbell's downward momentum and elastic bend. A sharp and forceful reversal synchronizes the athlete's force with the barbell's elastic rebound.

**Fault — Dipping too fast:** Separates the barbell from the shoulders (barbell's inertia prevents it from falling as fast). Must wait for the barbell to crash back — dissipates elastic energy. Or extending during the separation creates a collision. Both violate *timing* and risk injury.

**Fault — Dipping too slowly or hesitating:** Reduces barbell's downward momentum and elastic energy. Athletes may compensate by dipping deeper or extending longer.

**Coaching Tips:**
- If experiencing barbell separation during the dip, slow the dip speed.
- Incorporate heavy jerk dips for strength and familiarity with barbell oscillation.
- Use moderate weights with discs near the ends of the barbell (more oscillation) and follow the barbell's cadence. Or use hanging weights.
- Face a wall with toes touching, dip smoothly until patella touches the wall, then change direction aggressively.
- Plyometric training: box jumps, vertical jumps to strengthen elastic energy storage and transmission.

---

### Extension: Lower Body Coordination (Jerk)

**Standard:** Use ankles, knees, and hips to extend the whole body maximally, identical to the snatch and clean. Lower body initiates: push through the ground extending knees and hips simultaneously, maintaining ball-of-foot pressure. Immediately after, ankles plantarflex. Main difference from snatch/clean: shoulders push the barbell upward.

**Fault — Incomplete extension (splitting/squatting from dip position):** Legs cannot push powerfully, violating *fast*. Tensing the upper body further reduces barbell acceleration.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Jerk dips to build muscle memory for complete extension after descent.
- Jerk drive variations: pause at extension while keeping barbell connected to shoulders; jerk drives without arm push, allowing bar to lift off naturally.
- Imagine the extension as a vertical jump without leaving the ground.

---

### Catch: Internal Rotation Overhead (Jerk)

**Standard:** Internally rotate the arms as the barbell passes overhead. This stops the barbell's backward momentum and aligns it with the wrist, elbows, shoulders, and base of the neck. Maximizes overhead support by retracting back muscles and locking the arms.

**Fault — Following the barbell's diagonal trajectory (along forearms):** Barbell ends up behind the body; athletes arch the lower back to compensate. Stresses lower back and abs; barbell pulls on elbow joint. Violates *stable* and *close*.

**Fault — Continuing to externally rotate overhead:** Barbell travels a longer path; timing is affected. Catch may have bent arms, or arms extend then re-bend.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Point elbows behind you and squeeze shoulder blades as if holding a vertical pen with your mid-back.
- Imagine turning hands and arms away as if pressing against walls.

---

### Catch: Wrist Position Overhead

**Standard:** Extend the wrist and supinate the forearm while maintaining internal elbow rotation. Aligns barbell with wrist, elbow, shoulder, and base of neck (and hip during split jerk; midfoot during squat jerk). Creates external wrist torque that counters internal elbow torque — barbell rests on the base of the wrist.

**Fault — Wrist extended without torque:** Barbell in palm rather than base of wrist. Gap between barbell and arm violates *close*. Limits supportable weight. Torso may lean forward to balance.

**Fault — Neutral wrist position:** Unstable — high range of motion forward and backward. Can result from gripping tightly and/or changing rack position during the jerk dip.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Lift arm overhead, turn hand so thumb points toward you. Then turn away from the body so the base of the thumbs faces the ceiling.
- Imagine pushing wrists diagonally toward the ceiling.

---

## Split Jerk

### Extension: Upper Body Coordination (Split Jerk)

**Standard:** Push the barbell approximately 2/3 of the total distance the arms must travel to extend. For most athletes, elbows bend beyond 90° and the barbell reaches slightly above the forehead. Incorporates vertical force from ankles, knees, hips, and shoulders.

**Fault — Insufficient height:** Reduces time and space to split; barbell accumulates more downward momentum; arms may bend at catch. Athletes may compensate by bending knees deeper or sliding feet farther.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Incorporate jerk drives to practice extending to optimum height; use a partner with a stick or PVC in a squat rack.
- Imagine the extension as a vertical jump without leaving the ground.
- Imagine throwing the barbell to the ceiling from the shoulders.

---

### Catch: Foot Balance

**Standard:** Land on the ball of both feet during the split jerk. The ball of the foot is a natural braking mechanism. Front leg produces a horizontal backward force; back leg produces a horizontal forward force. Push off the ball of both feet equally to balance horizontal forces and keep the torso upright.

**Fault (general):** Shifting balance off the ball of one or both feet — many causes covered in the following sections.

**Coaching Tips:**
- The back leg technically lands first, but the split should feel simultaneous.
- Have a partner hold a PVC horizontally across the belly, touching lightly. If you lean into the PVC, push more with the front foot; if you have a gap, push more with the back foot.

---

### Catch: Back Leg Position

**Standard:** Bend the back knee maximally while pushing from the ball of the foot. This engages the rear hamstring and glute to push actively against the body — preventing falling backward and extending the rear leg to push upward against the barbell.

**Fault — Relaxed back knee:** Cannot push against bodyweight or the barbell's downward force; knee may hit the floor. Balance of rear foot shifts toward toes, reducing base. Athletes may compensate by flexing the front leg forward, causing barbell to move horizontally.

**Fault — Overextended back leg:** Moving backward during the dip/extension, or stepping too far with the back leg. Balance on the toes of the back leg pushes the torso forward and overloads the hip joint of the back leg.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine digging the ball of your foot into the ground.
- Push against the ground as hard as possible.
- Sled pushes/drags, reverse lunges into jerk position, press from split, uphill sprints.
- Strengthen back leg hamstrings: hamstring slides, glute bridges, single-leg deadlifts.

---

### Catch: Back Foot Position

**Standard:** Point the back foot toward the floor. This connects the entire metatarsal area with the ground for maximum stability, positions the rear leg and hip to extend against the barbell, and maintains a neutral pelvis.

**Fault — Back foot rotated outward:** Limits glute/adductor/hamstring contribution to hip extension; reduces forefoot-ground contact; can cause torso to rotate, placing lower back and spine in a vulnerable twisted position.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Place mats parallel on the outer sides of the feet; keep the lateral edge of feet in contact with mats as you split.
- Imagine pushing the floor away with the ball of the back foot.
- Static lunges with all toes contacting the ground and pointing forward; progress to overhead lunges.
- Perform presses in a proper split position for muscle memory.

---

### Catch: Front Leg Position

**Standard:** Maintain the front shin perpendicular to the floor while pushing from the ball of the foot. This allows the front leg to engage the quadriceps and push actively to prevent falling forward, and extends the front leg to push upward against the barbell.

**Fault — Shin pointing backward (heel-heavy):** Front foot overpowers the back leg, or the front foot steps too far. Balance shifts to heel, can lift toes, violates *stable*. Leg pushes against torso and hyperextends the lower back.

**Fault — Shin pointing forward (knee too far forward):** Moving forward during dip/extension or stepping too short. Front knee flexes more, balance on toes can lift the heel, violates *close* and *stable*. Athletes may hyperextend the lower back to compensate.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine digging the ball of the foot into the ground; pushing the ground away as if avoiding a fall.
- Set up a band horizontally across a squat rack; perform a split jerk so the knee touches the band lightly.
- Sled pulls backward to strengthen ball-of-foot pushing.

---

### Catch: Front Foot Position

**Standard:** Point the front foot forward. This produces the most force in the shortest time, connects the entire metatarsal area with the ground for lateral stability, and allows the front leg to provide maximal support.

**Fault — Landing on outer edge of front foot:** Reduces pressure on the inner edge; front knee may rotate outward past the foot to decelerate. Stresses the knee laterally/posteriorly; can cause lateral hip shift and pain.

**Fault — Landing on inner edge of front foot:** Can raise the lateral edge; reduces stability. Can overstress the medial knee, hip, and groin.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Use the same tips as for the back foot position.
- Reactive neuromuscular training (RNT) lunges in the split position.

---

### Catch: Leg Activation

**Standard:** Bend knees as much as possible while balancing on the ball of both feet. Front leg: push off ball of foot to contract front quadriceps. Back leg: push off ball of foot to contract rear hamstring and glute. Contractions should feel simultaneous and equal in magnitude to keep the torso vertical.

**Fault — Relaxed legs or excessive knee bend:** Balance of rear leg shifts forward to toes as knee drops; front knee flexes to compensate; heels may lift. Entire body shifts forward; forward/downward barbell momentum stresses shoulders and elbows.

**Fault — No knee flexion during catch:** Maximizes push against barbell but violates *low* — creates no room to catch as weight gets heavier.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine digging the ball of both feet into the ground.
- Imagine spreading the floor apart forcefully with the legs when they land.
- Perform static lunges or presses from split position with this leg action.

---

### Catch: Pelvis Position

**Standard:** Maintain a neutral pelvis in the split position. Allows rear glute/hamstring and front quadriceps to contract optimally, keeps the torso upright, facilitates core stabilization, and aligns hips/shoulders/elbows/wrists with the barbell.

**Fault — Posterior pelvic tilt:** Flexes spine, contracts abs excessively, moves torso away from barbell (violates *close*), shifts balance backward, can lift toes.

**Fault — Anterior pelvic tilt:** Hyperextends lower back, shifts torso forward (violates *close*), stretches abs (reduces stability under the bar). Arms may catch diagonally, stressing shoulders and elbows.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine the pelvis as a bowl of water — as you split, drop the bowl straight down without spilling.
- Have a partner hold a PVC across the abs; memorize the sensation; maintain it during the jerk. If pressing against the stick afterward, posteriorly rotate pelvis; if there is a gap, anteriorly rotate.

---

### Catch: Jerk Width

**Standard:** Split the legs while maintaining the hip-width stance established in the start position. This width allows legs to split along straight lines — the shortest and fastest path — and provides sufficient lateral stability.

**Fault — Narrower than hip-width (inward/diagonal split):** A diagonal path is longer than a straight path, even for the same split length. Arrives later, misses the barbell's apex. Excessive medial foot pressure; knees may cave inward.

**Fault — Wider than hip-width (outward/diagonal split):** Same timing issue, often worse because there is less physical limit to a wide split. Back leg is in a difficult position to bend. Stresses the groin.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Place mats parallel on the outer sides of feet; stay within these lines as you split.
- Focus on splitting straight forward and backward.
- Static lunges from optimal split position to build strength and muscle memory.
- Jerk dips and jerk drives to avoid rushing into split position.

---

### Catch: Jerk Symmetry

**Standard:** Split the legs symmetrically along the hip-width. The rear foot technically contacts first, but a symmetrical split reduces landing time between feet so it feels simultaneous, allowing both arms to push and catch at the same time.

**Fault — Upper body asymmetry:** Movement restrictions or strength imbalances preventing one arm from raising overhead fully. One side finishes before the other. Injury risk on both shoulders from asymmetrical loading.

**Fault — Rear leg splitting outward:** Increases time to finish the split, delays catching near the barbell's apex. Body may move laterally in the direction of the back leg, throwing off balance.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Single-arm dumbbell/kettlebell presses on the weaker side; use split position to incorporate the rear leg.
- Place mats on outer sides of feet; stay within them.
- Standing hip extension, single-leg back extension, reverse lunges, 5–10m sprints (ensure rear leg during takeoff matches rear leg during the jerk).
- Reverse lunges into the split position to focus on extending the leg directly behind.

---

### Catch: Head Position (Split Jerk)

**Standard:** Lower the head to 45° when supporting the barbell overhead. Allows the barbell to move backward and retracts back muscles to support the weight, aligning the barbell with wrist, elbow, shoulder, and hip joints.

**Fault — Head pushed forward:** Shifts torso forward; arm is diagonal rather than vertical relative to barbell. Forces the humerus forward, stressing shoulders and elbows, increasing catch instability.

**Fault — Looking up:** Shifts balance backward; extends neck and lower back near end range of motion; rotates shoulder blade downward, limiting arm elevation; barbell ends up in front of the head, overstressing shoulders.

**Coaching Tips:**
- If looking up: place tape on the ground ~1m away and try to look at it as you finish the catch.
- If leaning forward: have a partner hold a PVC across the abs; perform the jerk without leaning into the PVC.

---

### Split Jerk Recovery

**Standard:** Use two steps, each covering half the distance of the split — front foot recovers first (front leg is more flexed and conducive to extending), then back foot. After two steps, feet should align. This minimizes horizontal forces on the barbell and is the most stable recovery.

**Fault — One large step backward with front leg:** Distance is too long; requires backward momentum. If back heel drops, forward force disappears; easy to lose barbell behind. If back heel does not drop, balance is on ball of both feet with knees flexed — creates forward barbell momentum.

**Fault — Many small rapid steps backward:** Creates backward momentum; once back heel drops, further steps push balance to the heel.

**Fault — Large step forward with back leg:** Creates forward momentum; front leg stops its backward counterforce.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Perform split jerk recovery in a squat cage with barbell placed across one end; if balanced, barbell stays in contact with the cage.
- After the split, have a partner hold a stick across the lower back; if balanced, you will not push against the stick.

---

## Squat Jerk

### Extension: Upper Body Coordination (Squat Jerk)

**Standard:** Extend onto the ball of the foot and push the barbell to the height of the nose before descending. Lower than the split jerk because the squat jerk creates approximately seven times more momentum during the catch (the barbell drops a greater distance), so a lower height reduces stress. Incorporates vertical force of ankles, knees, hips, and shoulders.

**Fault — Extending but not pushing on the barbell (rushing to get under):** Barbell begins to descend sooner with more downward acceleration. If unable to reach squat position in time, cannot catch in a stable position.

**Fault — Pushing barbell too high:** Becomes a power jerk + overhead squat. Wastes energy and introduces instability. (Some athletes do this intentionally for power jerk training — coach must evaluate the tradeoff.)

**Coaching Tips:**
- Jerk drives to practice extending and throwing the barbell to nose height; use partner with stick or PVC in squat rack.
- Imagine the extension as a vertical jump without leaving the ground.

---

### Transition (Squat Jerk)

**Standard:** Actively dorsiflex ankles to slide feet downward and laterally into a back squat stance after the extension. Same stance regardless of catch depth — maximizes transfer between squat jerk, snatch, and clean. Feet finish sliding before lockout, but these movements should feel simultaneous.

**Fault — Changing stance based on depth:** Reduces transferability; muscle memory is biased toward the most practiced position, slowing overall development.

**Fault — Jumping (feet adjusted during upward phase):** Lengthens time in air. Violates *timing*; can prevent catching the barbell.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Imagine lowering the torso straight down, between the legs.
- Place tape on the side of the waist; focus on descending it vertically.
- Have a partner measure your back squat stance with discs/blocks/mats on the outer edges of the feet; perform power jerks, half jerks, and squat jerks so the outer edge of the feet lightly touches.

---

### Catch: Power Jerk

**Standard:** Flex knees and hips simultaneously with barbell balanced over the midfoot. Wrists, elbows, and shoulders align over the midfoot, maximizing support. Similarity to the snatch makes this position train both movements simultaneously.

**Fault — Knees flex without sufficient hip flexion:** Usually from backward lean during jerk dip or overextension. Torso stays vertical; only compensation is knee flexion. Stresses knees; may cause forward running during recovery. Some athletes hyperextend the back to compensate — relaxes abs and increases back stress.

**Fault — Hips flex without sufficient knee flexion:** False sense of getting under the barbell; stiff ankles. Creates diagonal arm alignment; shoulder impingement risk; dangerous if the barbell must be dropped.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Presses behind the neck at quarter squat height; power jerk recoveries; partial overhead squats in a squat rack; drop snatch to quarter squat height with jerk grip; power jerks with pause at catch.

---

### Catch: Head Position (Squat Jerk)

**Standard:** Lower the head to about 45° when supporting the barbell overhead. Brings the head slightly forward, moves the barbell backward, aligns the barbell over the midfoot, and retracts the back muscles to support the weight.

**Fault — Looking up during catch:** Compensates for difficulty placing the barbell over the base of the neck. Shifts load from back muscles to arms and shoulders. Can lead to neck/upper back hyperextension and overloading those areas.

**Fault — Head pushed excessively forward:** Shifts balance too far forward; flexes thoracic and lumbar spine; arms may end up diagonal to the barbell — shoulder stress.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Place tape on the ground ~1m away; finish the catch and look at the tape simultaneously; pause to adjust head position.
- Presses behind the neck at various squat heights; behind-the-neck push press; power jerk and squat jerk recoveries; behind-the-neck power jerk + overhead squat.

---

### Catch: Squat Balance

**Standard:** Align wrists, elbows, and shoulders with hips as close as possible to the ankles. Allows an upright and low squat, places load on quads/glutes/midback, brings the barbell as close to the ground as possible, and aligns it over the midfoot.

**Fault — Balance near ball of foot/toes:** Knees travel forward, heels may rise. Athletes lean back to compensate, reducing base and violating *stable*. Can result from forward-shifting jerk dip balance or lack of ankle mobility.

**Fault — Balance too far backward (heels):** Hips push back without knees going forward. Toes may lift. Leaning forward to compensate increases lower back stress and misaligns shoulders with barbell. Can result from balance issues in the jerk dip, overlong extension, or failure to internally rotate arms during catch.

**Coaching Tips:**
- See Jerk Dip sections for balance fixes.
- See Extension: Upper Body Coordination section for timing improvement.
- Overhead squat variations to the depth where balance begins to fail: on a box, with pauses, assisted by band looped around a squat rack. Also implement presses from front squat and back squat position to depth limits.

---

### Catch: Lower Back Tension (Squat Jerk)

**Standard:** Maintain the most upright posture while balancing the barbell over the midfoot. Keeps lower back rigid, transfers force from legs through the torso, and engages abdominals and glutes to stabilize against horizontal barbell movements. Critical because weight is much heavier in the squat jerk than in the snatch.

**Fault — Lower back rounds:** Can result from suboptimal jerk dip (off-balance trajectory); driving the barbell too high (more downward acceleration, athlete relaxes squat to increase depth). All violate *stable*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Strengthen anterior shin muscles: elevated split squats, narrow stance squats, step-ups to high box.
- Overhead squat variations with jerk grip to depth where lower back begins to round: partial squats, box squats, squats with hanging weights; behind-the-neck press at that depth.
- Squat holding a 5–10 kg disc in front to assist upright torso.

---

### Catch: Squat Width

**Standard:** Catch using the same back squat stance (~shoulder-width). Strongest for leg strength; allows the lowest possible catch, reduces barbell's downward momentum, maximizes stability, and brings the barbell closer to the ground.

**Fault — Stance too narrow (same as deadlift/jerk stance):** Thighs block the torso during the squat; athletes may default to a power jerk or half jerk — violates *low*.

**Fault — Stance too wide:** Lack of coordination in jerk dip/extension to perform a deep squat; or slow reversal reduces barbell momentum, requiring longer extension but less time to squat under. A wider stance compensates for depth but violates *timing* and *low*.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Extend on the ball of the foot, then slide into squat stance.
- If more comfortable in a different stance than the overhead squat, train the overhead squat in the squat jerk stance (or vice versa).
- Place mats on outer edges of feet at squat width; stay within those lines.
- Jerk dips to build rhythm; squat jerks with discs near bar ends to increase elasticity and build rhythm with light weights.

---

### Catch: Knee Alignment (Squat Jerk)

**Standard:** Turn feet outward and align with knees to push through the midline of the foot. Exact angle depends on hip structure, thoracic mobility, and ankle mobility.

**Fault — Knees too far out past lateral foot edge:** May compensate for an excessively narrow squat stance. Reduces lateral base and makes recovery more difficult. Violates *stable*.

**Fault — Knees rotating inward:** From relaxing the squat to increase the time to stop the barbell; or from an excessively wide stance that prevents sufficient knee external rotation and dorsiflexion. Stresses knees; relaxes lower back; reduces recovery power.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Narrow-grip overhead box squats at various heights (quarter, half, full); progress to paused narrow-grip overhead squats; power jerk recoveries and squat jerk recoveries; then 3-level jerk to gradually lower depth.

---

### Catch: Rotation (Squat Jerk)

**Standard:** Catch the barbell evenly to minimize rotational movement. Because the squat jerk has a narrower grip than the snatch, the stabilizing lever is shorter and requires more effort. Keeps barbell over midfoot, preserves balance, and disperses load evenly.

**Fault — Overhead rotation:** Can result from asymmetry in the rack position or jerk dip — one elbow higher, or asymmetrical arm elevation/shoulder rotation. The stronger side absorbs more load while the weaker side is in a suboptimal position.

**Coaching Tips:**
- Follow suggestions from Jerk Dip: Elbow Symmetry and Jerk Dip: Depth sections.
- Have a partner hold a PVC vertically along one side of the barbell; maintain consistent contact during: partial narrow-grip overhead squats; overhead squats to a box; barbell presses with jerk grip at various back squat heights.

---

### Squat Jerk Recovery

**Standard:** Lift the chest aggressively and contract the glutes to keep the barbell moving vertically over the midfoot as the athlete stands up. Hips and shoulders rise together. Head maintains 45° downward tilt.

**Fault — Hips move backward:** Cannot extend hips properly at the bottom of the squat — knees, thighs, and foot pressure collapse inward. Hips shift backward; head moves forward to create balance. Torso inclines forward; requires greater shoulder flexion; usually results in raising the hips further.

**Coaching Tips:**
- After catching, have a partner place a stick vertically aligned with the midfoot; maintain barbell contact with the stick while standing.
- Narrow-grip overhead box squats at various heights with a pause to emphasize the recovery; progress to narrow-grip overhead paused squats; add bands around the knees if knees collapse.
- Place tape on the side of the waist; focus on lifting it vertically during recovery.
