## What Is the Soviet System?

The Soviet System is a **holistic approach to training for the Olympic sport of weightlifting**. It includes a scientific approach to designing training programs, a philosophical basis, and recovery systems.

It was developed in the Soviet Union (USSR) in the 20th century by Soviet researchers, as well as researchers from other communist countries, particularly Cuba.

It produced hundreds of Olympic medals, world championship medals, and world records. It is still in use today, continuing to generate weightlifting glory.

**The Soviet system is the basis for all modern weightlifting systems.** Many coaches in modern Russia, China, North Korea, and Cuba use the Soviet System. Other highly successful countries like Bulgaria, Kazakhstan, Greece, and Colombia are either derivative of the Soviet system or use it for developing athletes prior to the Olympic level (Ivan Rojas, 2015).

## Founders of the Soviet System

The two main founders of the Soviet System were:

- **Arkady Nikitich Vorobiev** (1924–2012)
- **Alexey Medvedev Sidorovich** (1927–2003)

Both were lifters in the Soviet Union who later became coaches and programming theorists. Although they started from similar training backgrounds, their approach to programming and training became nuancedly different.

### Vorobiev

Arkady Vorobiev won **two Olympic gold medals** in 1956 and 1960 and was a **four-time World Champion** (1954, 1955, 1957, 1958). He set 21 middle-heavyweight world records — nine in snatch, three in clean and jerk, two in press, and seven in the total (Herrera, 2012).

In addition to being a weightlifter, Vorobiev was a remarkable scientist who began his scientific achievements while still a competitive athlete:
- Graduated from the Medical Institute during his sports career
- Defended a Candidate of Science degree in 1962
- Defended a Doctor of Medical Science degree in 1970 at the Institute of Aviation and Space Medicine in Moscow
- Rector of the Moscow Oblast Institute of Physical Culture and Sports (1977)
- Elected to the International Weightlifting Federation Hall of Fame (1995)

His research areas included the application of weightlifting training to cosmonauts, studying protocols to reduce muscle atrophy in space.

### Medvedev

Alexey Medvedev Sidorovich (1927–2003) won the world title in the heavyweight category in 1957 and 1958. He was the first Soviet weightlifter and third in the world to reach a total of 500 kg (Herrera, 2012). Note: during his career, prior to 1976, there were 3 lifts — snatch, clean and jerk, and clean and press.

In 1962, Medvedev became the coach of the national weightlifting team of the USSR. In 1964, he prepared 3 Olympic champions for the USSR. He worked as head of weightlifting at the Russian State University of physical culture, sport and tourism, preparing specialists not only for Russia but from Cuba, China, Poland, Bulgaria, and North Korea.

He traveled to China many times, lecturing at Beijing University of Physical Culture. He received the title of Honorary Professor of Beijing University of Physical Culture — the first weightlifter to receive this honorary degree.

## The Architecture of the Soviet System

The goal of the Soviet System is to increase competitive results — in other words, increase a weightlifter's total at competitions.

The system's elements include:

- **Concepts:** The scientific disciplines leveraged — mechanics, mathematics, biology, and biochemistry.
- **Objects:** The actual things the weightlifter and coach do to increase performance — training program, recuperation systems, and nutrition. A successful training program must be managed to the individual lifter, and its success depends on a good diet and intentional rest.
- **Subjects:** The people applying the concepts and carrying out the objects.

## Philosophy of the Soviet System

### Hegel's Dialectic

The Soviet System uses the basic principles of Hegel's dialectic — **thesis, antithesis, and synthesis**. A theorist would come up with a thesis (new idea). Another theorist would have an opposing antithesis. The goal was to synthesize the two opposing ideas into a new, more effective idea. Later, this synthesis would become a new thesis, and the cycle of knowledge production could start again.

In other words, the Soviets cared about what worked and what could be proven to work — further explained by their embracing of **realism, pragmatism, and materialism of natural science**.

### Pragmatism

The Soviet system was essentially pragmatic. If a theory worked, it was accepted and applied. Researchers and coaches had freedom to try different approaches as long as they got results. As long as the science did not criticize the ideology of the central government (historic materialism), the research was permitted (Rosenthal, 1964).

### Realism

The real world is whatever exists outside of subjectivity, outside of your mind — independent of what you believe the real world is (Rosenthal, 1964).

### Materialism of Natural Science

Scientists interpret nature based on practical data and experimentation. For something to exist, it must be able to be empirically proven. All facts can be scientifically proven (Rosenthal, 1964).

**In summary:** the material presented in this book is the result of rigorous scientific research. Anything presented as a fact was at some point proven via an experiment. The programming and recovery systems were used on real athletes to produce real results, such as Olympic Gold medals.

## Strategies and Methods for Increasing Competitive Sports Performance

The following strategies and methods are aimed at increasing the weightlifter's total at a competition. They were developed on research, for the most part, on Soviet adult male weightlifters, and have been applied successfully to female weightlifters. Many can also benefit strength athletes in general.

To increase competitive sports results, the athlete must increase their muscle strength and their body's ability to produce said strength.

### Static Maximum Strength

Static Maximum Strength is the maximum force a muscle group can apply. In weightlifting, the movements are dynamic and executed in seconds. Static strength, by itself, is an unreliable indicator of genetic potential in weightlifters — many strong athletes fail to become successful weightlifters because they are not explosive enough.

The increase in static strength is not proportional with increases in the Snatch and Clean and Jerk (Herrera, 2012). For example, weightlifters can apply the equivalent of lifting 500kg of static maximum strength in the beginning of the second phase of the pull — far more than any competition lift.

**For training program design:** static strength is an output or side benefit of training, not the core focus. Static strength training systems (e.g., powerlifting) are not important for weightlifters.

### Relative Strength

Relative strength is a measure of how much an athlete lifts in comparison to their size. It has a **direct relation with increase of sports quality** (Herrera, 2012).

The Soviets empirically established the expected height for each bodyweight class. An ideal height-to-weight relationship for each class was established at different skill levels. The data shows that athletes at a higher skill level will typically have a smaller height for the same body mass — expected as the athlete puts on more lean muscle mass and increases bone density as they progress from beginner to Olympic level.

*Note: A new chart for women has not yet been formally created as of the Houston 2015 World Championships, but it is speculated that slightly higher heights relative to bodyweight will occur due to anthropological differences between male and female skeletal structure.*

### Relation of Strength Between Flexor and Extensor Muscles

With the increase of quality training, the ratio of strength between the flexor and extensor muscles is enhanced. Empirical studies show the following desirable ratios for weightlifters (Herrera, 2012):

| Muscle Group Pair | Desirable Ratio (Flexor:Extensor) |
|---|---|
| Knee flexors:extensors | 60:100 |
| Hip flexors:extensors | 80:100 |
| Trunk flexors:extensors | 80:100 |
| Elbow flexors:extensors | 70:100 |
| Shoulder flexors:extensors | 60:100 |

### Strength Applied on Time: Muscle Power

Power has a close relationship with competitive results, particularly for evaluating and forecasting competition performance. It is evaluated by jumping — either long jump or vertical jump.

For example, Olympic Champion Yuri Vardanian's highest competition result correlated with his static high jump of 3 feet 4 inches and a long jump of 11 feet (Herrera, 2012).

### Frequency and Quality of Nerve Impulses

For the increase of competition performance, not only the quantity of muscle mass is important — the **optimal frequency of nerve impulses** and the capacity to recruit motor units quickly are also important (Herrera, 2012).

This varies depending on muscle fatigue, and is especially crucial in the 90–100% of maximum intensity zone. The sample programs in this book employ optimal repetition ranges in consideration of this.

### Excitability of the Central Nervous System

The ability to place the central nervous system in an excited state was shown to increase strength over baseline by **10%**, translating to increased competition results. This increase results from the segregation of hormones of quick action such as adrenaline and noradrenaline, as well as the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (Herrera, 2012).

Experimentation showed that training with medium to high loading caused:
- Quantity of noradrenaline in the blood to double
- Quantity of HGH to increase
- Increase in blood cortisone levels
- Hormones from the supra-adrenal glands (including corticoids) to increase their presence in the blood

### Hormonal Changes

Hormonal changes have direct influence in catabolism or recovery. Optimally leveraging hormonal changes leads to increasing the training loading and as a collateral result, the increase of muscle mass (Herrera, 2012).

Synthetic hormones have been used, but their secondary effects are harmful and are banned and penalized by WADA and the COI (Herrera, 2012).

Researchers also look for permissible ways to optimize hormones naturally. Permissible areas include:
- **Growth hormone:** affected by amino acids, particularly lysine, ornithine, and arginine. Carnitine has also been used widely.
- **Steroid hormones** (such as Vitamin D, estrogen, pregnenolone, and testosterone): affected by various mechanisms. Comprehensive bloodwork is recommended to determine which natural supplements may aid in optimizing natural hormone levels. Supplementation must be individualized.

## Means and Methodology to Optimize the Training Loading

The key methods are:
- Daily training regime
- Effect of time of day on motor capabilities
- Recovery (Sleep, Nutrition)
- Training in conditions of moderated hypoxia
- Influence of cold — an integral view

### Daily Training Regime

Adaptation of the athlete is a cumulative process, where training loads are combined with rest periods. This is one of the Russian principles of daily training: **"relation between training and rest"** (Herrera, 2012). Daily training and rest are the foundation of competitive performance.

### Effect of Time of Day on Motor Capabilities

Motor capabilities during the day have peaks and troughs, depending on climate, phylogenetic factors, and lifestyle (Herrera, 2012):

| Time of Day | Best Motor Capability |
|---|---|
| Early morning (~6am) | Aerobic activity |
| Around 10am | Speed and strength |
| After lunch | Capabilities go down |
| Early afternoon | Flexibility work |
| Late afternoon | Strength and especially explosive strength |

**Practical application:**
- Morning: light aerobic exercises, then resistance force work (squats and pulls)
- Afternoon: explosive lifts (snatch and C&J)
- Third session (if applicable): isolated exercises, specifically antagonistic muscles

### Recovery

#### Sleep

Sleep is a fundamental time in which recovery of muscle tissue occurs. It is advised to sleep **7–8 hours a day** and **30 minutes after lunch** (Herrera, 2012).

### Nutrition

Caloric expenditure during training can vary between 200 and 800 kilocalories (Herrera, 2012). Weightlifting does not burn as many calories as other Olympic sports. However, the exhaustion of phosphates is high (Herrera, 2012).

Key nutritional guidelines:
- **Protein:** the most important macro-nutrient. Advised 1 gram of protein per kilogram of body weight. Preferred sources: milk and its products, meat, fish, and natural gelatin (which contains glutamine — the amino acid with the most significant presence in muscle, essential for tissue recovery).
- **Carbohydrates and fats** should be controlled to avoid increase in body fat composition.
- An amino acid panel ordered by a licensed medical professional is advised to determine which amino acids the athlete's diet and supplementation is missing.

### Training in Conditions of Moderated Hypoxia

Training at high altitudes for short periods of time can assist in improving athletic performance. A training period of **3 weeks at moderate altitude (7,874 feet / ~2,400m above sea level)** has shown an influence in increase of maximum strength between **10% and 30%** (Herrera, 2012). Elite athletes train at this altitude for 21-day periods.

High altitude training is ideal for focused training camps in the preparatory phases of training — it is not necessary to train at high altitude all year long.

### Influence of Cold — an Integral View

Recent research has contradicted the long-held belief that icing muscles improves recovery, citing that inflammation can be good for muscle repair. However, it is important not to take a reductionist view — the following sections examine cryotherapy from an integral perspective, describing its use in the Soviet System and the biological mechanisms it likely affects.

#### The Primary Goal of Icing Is to Create Beneficial, Natural Hormonal Changes

Theoretically, thermic stimulation speeds the recovery process by increasing local blood circulation and stimulating the hormonal system (Herrera, 2012). Experimentation has shown that cold showers between weightlifting exercises increased work capability for the next exercise (Herrera, 2012).

Practical applications from the Soviet System:
- Cold application to affected areas at the **end of each workout** to prevent injuries
- At high-level training: **thermic contrast — alternating cold and hot** (Herrera, 2012)
- In Russian training centers: athletes finish training with **sauna and cold water pools**, often at the end of the training week (Rojas, 2014)
- **Icing the kidney area** for 15 minutes after training has shown positive results (Herrera, 2012) — not surprising as adrenal glands are located by the kidneys
- Cuban athletes rubbed ice on legs, shoulders, and low back between exercises (Rojas, 2014)
- Cuban Olympic gold medalist Daniel Nunez credits his 20 world records and Olympic gold medal to ice therapy (Nunez, 2014)

#### Inductive Reasoning: Hormonal Mechanisms for Increased Performance Due to Cold

Cold clearly impacts the adrenal system, which in turn impacts the athlete's ability to train. Key findings:

- **Short-term cold exposure** can increase cortisol and norepinephrine (Paakkonen & Leppaluoto, 2002) — both essential for athletes
- Even exposure to cold air for just **5 minutes at 4°C** raised plasma norepinephrine (Paakkonen & Leppaluoto, 2002)
- **Norepinephrine** is a stress hormone triggering the fight-or-flight response. In weightlifting, the sympathetic nervous system is used to execute maximal lifts in a reflex-like reaction. It causes increased heart rate and enables muscles to work faster and more efficiently. It also increases glucose, an energy source for the muscles.
- **Cortisol** is needed to deal with the stress of training and inflammation. Ideally, cortisol levels should follow a circadian rhythm and training times are optimally arranged around the athlete's circadian rhythm.
- Scientists have proven that cold exposure can allow an athlete to exert **direct control over the sympathetic nervous system** (Anne Houtman, 2016). Wim Hof was found to be able to influence his immune response and the hormones epinephrine, norepinephrine, and cortisol with cold exposure combined with breathing exercises and meditation.

**Caution:** Long-term exposure to cold can decrease thyroid hormones T3 free and T4 free (Paakkonen & Leppaluoto, 2002). The Soviet and Cuban methods used cold exposure under **short durations**.

**Summary:** Cold exposure can increase norepinephrine and epinephrine (readying the body for the next exercise) and increase cortisol (to deal with inflammation and enable continuation at a high performance level).

#### Direct Experience with Ice Therapy at Risto Sports

In the training of Rocio Navarro, a member of the Panamanian National Team in the 69kg weight category, ice therapy combined with Soviet System programming enabled her to advance from a national level lifter to an elite level Pan American lifter.

Coach Ivan significantly increased her volume and introduced daily ice therapy on the legs after every workout. In a **6-month period**:

| Metric | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Back squat | 145 kg | 175 kg |
| Snatch | 83 kg | 92 kg |
| Clean & jerk | 107 kg | 115 kg (training: 119 kg) |

This represents a **~13% improvement** — remarkable considering that mature international athletes realistically seek 4.8% improvement per year. She was subsequently able to out-lift Cuban and Venezuelan lifters, both countries with long traditions of elite weightlifting.

The keys to her improvement were: (1) religious use of ice therapy after every training session, and (2) increased training volume using the Soviet System.

## Dynamic and Kinematic Structure of the Lift

The lift is divided into phases — true for both the snatch and the clean and jerk.

The kinematics of the lift show the relationships between force and velocity through the phases:

- **Minimum force** occurs as the bar passes the knees
- **Maximum force** occurs as the athlete starts their final extension of the pull
- **Peak velocity** occurs at the top of the pull, while force almost drops rapidly — reaching zero shortly thereafter

Physically: the lifter applies maximal force, which causes the bar to reach maximum velocity. As bar velocity peaks, the lifter is going under the bar. As the lifter turns over and goes under the bar, the force drops to zero — the bar is still travelling upward while the lifter's body is no longer imparting force on it; in a sense, the lifter is in free fall.

### Phases of the Snatch

The snatch is composed of **Actions, Periods, and Phases** (Lukashov 1972, Miulberg 1988). These scientifically accepted terms enable coaches and lifters to share ideas internationally. A perfect snatch is one continuous motion, particularly the pull.

**Three Actions:**

#### Preparatory Action
Focuses on the start position of the lifter and ends when the bar lifts off the platform. The start position can be either:
- **Dynamic:** the lifter moves their hips into the bar and gives a final impulse to help initiate lift-off. A pulse of energy is transferred by quickly lowering the hips forward then straight into the lift-off motion. It requires precision — very easy to mistime. Lifters like Nikolai Peschalov have used this to win Olympic medals.
- **Static:** the lifter lowers down into the start position, flexing the muscles of the legs, then goes into the lift-off. Stretching down into the hamstrings stores energy similarly to a compressed spring. This position tends to be more repeatable, leading to fewer technical errors. Naim Suleymanoglu, one of the most successful weightlifters of all time, used a static start position.

#### Principal Action
Contains the **pull** and **turnover** periods — both of which can make or break a lift. Broken down into phases:
- **Lift-off:** bar coming off the floor
- **Previous impulse:** energy transmitted to the bar prior to passing the knees
- **Shock absorption:** passing the knees
- **Final impulse:** final burst of energy transmitted before the bar turns over
- **Turnover (phases 10–11):** bar travels upward, unsupported by the lifter, enabling the lifter to slide their feet out. Similar to a baseball player throwing a ball straight up — the bar has enough energy imparted that it travels upward without further action, though the lifter's hands remain connected.
- **Catch (phase 13):** feet are fully contacting the floor, the lifter has caught the bar in an overhead squat position.

#### Final Action
Consists of the **recovery** and **fixation** periods — the athlete stands up with the bar and holds it overhead for the down signal.

### Phases of the Clean and Jerk

The clean follows the same action structure as the snatch (Preparatory, Principal, Final). The jerk also has Preparatory, Principal, and Final actions, with the **prelude period** markedly different from the pull periods of the snatch and clean:

- **Semi-flexion phase:** the athlete dips downward with the bar by bending their knees
- **Breaking phase:** the athlete changes direction with their dip — the point where they stop descending
- **Thrust phase:** the athlete explodes out of the dip and moves the bar upward

After the thrust, the athlete slides under the bar into a split (or squat/power jerk position), supports the bar overhead, then brings their feet together and stands up with the bar fully overhead (recovery and fixation).

### Executing Exercises With a Different Rhythm to Increase Results

In practice, **60% of the best Soviet lifters** considered it useful to execute squats, pulls, and bench press with a **slow rhythm every 7–10 days** at the end of their training sessions (Herrera, 2012). The pull could be done with a slow return.

**Important:** It would be highly detrimental to execute technical exercises (snatch and clean and jerk) at different rhythms. The goal in training is to make technique — including speed — as repeatable and reflex-like as possible. Varying rhythm applies only to strength exercises.

A Soviet study on varying the rhythm of the squat and its effect on squat PR found (Lelokov):

| Squat Rhythm | Average Improvement | Std Dev |
|---|---|---|
| Varying rhythm | Highest | Second lowest |
| Fast rhythm | Lowest | — |
| Medium rhythm | Second highest | Lowest |

**Practical application:** Schedule a change in squat pace every 7–10 days, or simply change squat rhythm between repetitions or sets during daily training. If it is too difficult to modulate rhythm, apply medium speed — found to be the second most effective method with the lowest standard deviation of the 5 rhythms tested.

## Creating a Training Program

### Key Definitions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Organism** | The subject or, in this case, the athlete |
| **Training Load** | The group of stimuli that causes a chain reaction of the functional systems in the organism. Effectivity is valued by the depth and duration of changes in the organism (Herrera, 2012) |
| **Repetitions (Reps)** | Each lift completed for a particular exercise |
| **Set** | A group of repetitions completed without significant rest |
| **Volume** | The indicator of quantity of stimuli (repetitions, kg lifted, kilometers, kilocalories) |
| **Intensity** | The indicator of quality of stimuli or level of difficulty, determined basically by the maximum result (Herrera, 2012). Calculated as the percent of the athlete's maximum result |
| **Personal Record (PR)** | The athlete's best result in any lift. Also known as their "max" |
| **Max Out (Maxing-Out)** | When the athlete attempts to lift 100% or more of their personal record in training |

### Intensity Zones

Before discussing program design, intensity zones must be defined. Intensity is segregated into **seven zones** (Herrera, 2012):

| Zone | % of PR |
|---|---|
| Zone 1 | ≤ 50% (not counted in training volume) |
| Zone 2 | 50–59% |
| Zone 3 | 60–69% |
| Zone 4 | 70–79% |
| Zone 5 | 80–89% |
| Zone 6 | 90–99% |
| Zone 7 | 100%+ |

**Important:** The Soviets do not count repetitions done at less than 50% of a lifter's PR towards the training cycle's volume. Work with the bar and light weight should always be done before a training session for warming up and improving technique, but does not count toward programmed volume.

Depending on which cycle the lifter is in, the lifter will do most work in a particular intensity zone.

### Training Cycle Design

The weightlifter's program is designed in **yearly blocks**, then broken down into **3-month blocks (mesocycles)**, then into **week-by-week blocks (microcycles)**. Workouts where the weightlifter attempts greater than 95% of their best lifts are planned events.

**Volume and intensity are the two key parameters in programming design** — a delicate balance between the two.

Volume and intensity follow a cyclic, sinusoidal pattern: as one increases, the other decreases. This dance between parameters is what creates a champion's program.

### Cycles and Intensity

The training year consists of four cycle types:

| Cycle | Duration | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Macrocycle** | ~1 year | Full annual cycle, broken into 4 mesocycles |
| **Mesocycle** | ~3 months (12 weeks) | Quarterly block; contains 3 phases |
| **Microcycle** | 1 week | Weekly training block |
| **Training Session** | 1 session | Individual workout |

**Three phases of the mesocycle**, always in this order (they sequentially build on each other):

**Preparatory phase (weeks 1–7):** Prepares the lifter's body for handling maximal loads. Creates adaptation to high demands. Higher volume, lower intensity. One test point.

**Pre-competitive phase (weeks 8–9):** Continues preparing the body for competition. The body can handle increased intensity while volume is maintained. One test point.

**Competitive phase (weeks 10–12):** Final phase before competition. Volume is decreased, intensity increases to its greatest. 2–3 test points where the lifter is prepared to max out.

**Test points (control points / max-outs) by week:**
- **Week 7:** Lifter can go up to 100%; not necessarily seeking to exceed PR
- **Week 9:** Lifter may go above 100%; first test point where new PRs are sought
- **Week 12:** Lifter must hit as high a max as possible — ideally aligned with the most important competition

### Strategic Planning

By planning in 12-week cycles, the athlete's body adapts to peak at more predictable times. This is a huge advantage — the coach and/or athlete can predict when the athlete will be ready to hit a big personal record.

**Key principles:**
- The most important competitions should fall at the end of each 12-week cycle (week 12 of the final mesocycle of the year)
- Secondary meets (qualifiers for the primary competition) should fall on week 12 of prior mesocycles
- It is customary to compete at least 2 times before week 12 — usually weeks 7 and 10
- It is important to compete as much as possible when intensity is planned to be over 95% — PR's should not be left in the training hall
- A training camp is often scheduled prior to the actual competition

**Note on exercise intensity:** The three primary exercises — snatch, clean and jerk, and squat — do not always have the same intensity zones worked. Some weeks both the snatch and clean and jerk are maxed; some weeks the squat and clean and jerk are maxed.

### Volume

The program is conceived by setting a goal for the **total volume of the macrocycle** — the total repetitions the lifter will complete for the entire quarter-year.

Volume is then decomposed:
1. Set the macrocycle total volume
2. Split it over the 4 mesocycles (sinusoidally — up one mesocycle, down the next)
3. Split it over each microcycle within each mesocycle
4. Spread it over each training session per day

**Within the macrocycle:** The highest volume mesocycle is typically at the beginning.

**Within each mesocycle:** The preparatory phase accounts for the most volume, with volume reducing through pre-competitive and competitive phases as intensity increases.

**Within each microcycle:** Volume and intensity follow a sinusoidal pattern. Even on de-load weeks:
- First day of the week: lowest percent of max for the week
- Volume drops mid-week
- Highest percent of max lifts are done on the last day of the week with moderate to higher volume

**Intensity variation within the week:** Per the Kazakhstan Weightlifting System (Ivan Rojas, 2015), the highest intensity is in the beginning and end of the week.

### How to Calculate Total Volume

Using an example with a lifter whose maximum is 100kg:

1. **Total repetitions:** multiply reps per set × number of sets at each weight, then sum across all weights
2. **Total kilograms lifted:** weight value × reps × sets at each weight, then sum
3. **Medium weight:** total kilos lifted ÷ total reps
4. **Relative medium intensity:** medium weight ÷ lifter's max

Example result: 23 total reps, 1,657 kg total, medium weight = 72, relative medium intensity = 72%.

Depending on the mesocycle and the lifter, the coach will aim for a different relative medium intensity value.

### How to Spread the Volume

A simplified example using 3,000 total repetitions for a macrocycle:

**Step 1:** Divide by number of mesocycles (3):
- 3,000 ÷ 3 = 1,000 reps per mesocycle

**Step 2:** Skew toward higher volume in preparatory phase, lower in competitive phase:
- Preparatory phase: 1,500 reps
- Pre-competitive phase: 1,000 reps
- Competitive phase: 500 reps

**Step 3:** Split within each mesocycle (example: pre-competitive, 4 weeks):
- 1,000 ÷ 4 = 250 reps/week average
- Apply sinusoidal variation: week 5: 350, week 6: 200, week 7: 150 (max-out week), week 8: 300

**Step 4:** Spread within the week (example: week 7, 6-day training):
- 150 ÷ 6 = 25 reps/day average
- Apply intensity variation: Day 1: 25, Day 2: 33, Day 3: 23, Day 4: 17, Day 5: 30, Day 6: 22

*Note: the numbers in this example are far lower than a typical cycle for a competitive lifter — they are used to illustrate the method with easily manipulated numbers.*

### Selecting the Right Number of Training Sessions per Week

The Soviet System can be applied to any level of training. Before writing a program, establish the level of the lifter:

| Level | Description | Recommended Sessions |
|---|---|---|
| **1a — Beginner/Recreational/Master** | Starting lifters; competing only locally; most Masters | 3–4 days/week, 1 session/day |
| **1b — Intermediate** | Competes locally with fair results; has not yet qualified for major national event | 4–5 days/week; possibly 6 with one double session |
| **1c — Advanced** | Competes at major national competitions | 6 days/week |

**Lifestyle categories for advanced (1c) lifters:**
- **A:** Works, studies, has a family, and lifts
- **B:** Works or studies, and lifts
- **C:** Works or studies part-time, and lifts
- **D:** Only lifts (ideal — can completely arrange life around training)

Case D lifters should train 6 days/week with at least 8–10 training sessions/week. Some full-time lifters report up to 12 sessions/week.

**The coach must be realistic about the actual number of sessions and days per week the lifter can truly train. The lifter must be open and honest about this and commit to a set number of training days per mesocycle.**

### Organizing a Training Log

The daily training should be recorded in a log. Data recorded by the **lifter**: date, bodyweight, heartbeat, and any missed lifts. The training log otherwise assumes all lifts were successful. The lifter may also note when exercises felt particularly easier than normal or when they had a breakthrough.

Data entered by the **coach**: lifter's name, cycle information, percentages, repetitions, best recorded lifts, average weight, tonnage, and average intensity zone.

It is important to note the macro, meso, and microcycle number and date. This allows comparative analysis — for example, if a lifter completes less than 90% of their lifts for a given microcycle, the coach can look at the same microcycle of the previous macrocycle to see if the lifter struggled similarly, and then alter their approach.

### Adjusting the Individual's Training Plan

#### Missing Lifts

It is expected that a lifter will miss some lifts during a cycle. Generally:
- Lifts missed **behind** are counted as lifted in training
- Lifts missed **out front or otherwise** are considered true misses
- The lifter is **not expected to save lifts** in training (for safety)

**The coach should be concerned if the lifter misses more than 10% of their lifts for a given week.**

Approaches for greater than 10% misses:
- Document which exercises had misses
- Review bodyweight metrics — loss of more than 1 kg/week can indicate insufficient recovery (*countermeasure: improve diet and recovery*)
- Review whether the PR numbers used for percentages are accurate — an inflated PR makes the program unsuccessful (*countermeasure: adjust max, recalculate*)
- Ensure the athlete is reporting any injuries (*countermeasure: seek medical care*)
- Check technique for drift (*countermeasure: add 30 min technical work with bar at the beginning of each session*)
- If all the above seems normal: consider dropping intensity by a few percent while keeping the repetitions the same
- **Always lower intensity first, before lowering volume**
- If necessary, lower volume as a last resort

**Important rule: when lifters are missing lifts, never lower the volume without first lowering the intensity. This is to keep the lifter safe.**

#### New PRs During the Macrocycle

It is of utmost importance that the **same maxes or personal records are used to calculate the percentages for the entire macrocycle**. If a lifter hits a new personal record in week 9, for example, the coach still uses the previous maxes from the beginning of the cycle to finish out the 12-week macrocycle.

## Case Study: Russia, China, and Bulgaria — Reason for Chinese Supremacy

A common misconception is that Bulgarians, in their heyday, maxed out all the time. Is this true?

This section examines data comparing the training of China, Bulgaria, and Russia for female lifters in the late 1990s (1998).

### Background

Throughout the 1990s, all three countries were forces in women's lifting. In the earlier 1990s, Russia had more dominance. By the end of the 1990s, China was the undisputed best country in women's weightlifting, sweeping gold medals in almost any weight class they competed in. Bulgaria, for its incredibly small size and population, routinely had medalists and/or champions.

**How did China overtake both Russia and Bulgaria?**

**Key finding on volume:** In this time period, Russia had dropped the number of repetitions its female weightlifters were doing annually. Meanwhile, China's coaches had increased the number of repetitions — most concentrated in the snatch and clean and jerk. The **increase in repetitions correlated with China overtaking Russia and Bulgaria**.

**Key finding on intensity:** Internet folklore suggests the Bulgarians do the highest intensity of all systems. However, looking at the data:
- The Bulgarian intensity coefficient was only **3% higher** than the Chinese intensity coefficient and approximately the same percent higher than the Russian coefficient
- The differences in intensities between the systems are **not significant** — they can be considered almost the same

**Paradoxically:** Upon examining snatch repetitions done at 90% or more, the Chinese actually did **more repetitions than the Bulgarians**. This is easily explained by the mesocycle phase — in the Soviet System, work in higher intensity zones increases towards the end of the macrocycle in the pre-competitive and competitive phases.

**Conclusion:** The primary driver of Chinese supremacy was not higher intensity — it was **greater total volume**, particularly in the competition lifts.

## Notes on Applicability to Other Sports

The Soviet System works for anything. It is fundamentally a delicate balance between reps and intensity, as well as work and recovery. To apply this system to another activity, simply replace the exercises with the exercises of the new activity.

Dr. Herrera once applied the Soviet System to managing the workload of the chief designer of the Russian MiG (Mikoyan), who had been suffering deleterious health effects from overwork. Dr. Herrera created a "training program" for the chief designer, varying his workload day-to-day and week-to-week, similarly to how loading is varied in a weightlifting program — resulting in positive health effects.

**The principles of the Soviet System can be applied to anything that involves a defined workload to be done over a defined period of time.**

## Sample Programs — Nomenclature

The sample programs are 12-week programs (1 mesocycle), divided into 3 to 6 days per week. The following examples explain how to read the nomenclature used.

### Example One: Sets by Reps
`Snatches 70 (2x3)`
→ Snatches at 70% of max for 2 sets of 3 repetitions.

### Example Two: Power Clean and Power Jerk Complexes
`P. Clean + 3PJ 60%(3x1-1) 65%(3x1-2)`
→ At 60% of max: 1 power clean, 3 power jerks for 1 set. At 65% of max: 1 power clean, 3 power jerks for 2 sets.

### Example Three: Reps, Only 1 Set
`Back Squat 70%x4 80%x3`
→ Back squat 70% of max for 4 reps for 1 set, then back squat 80% of max for 3 reps for 1 set.

### Example Four: Back Squat Plus Jerk Behind Neck
`Back Squat + Jerk 45% (2x3)`
→ Back squat 45% of max for 2 sets of 3 reps each in the back squat and jerk. Done as: one back squat followed by a jerk, repeated for 3 total pairs.

### Example Five: Power Clean, Front Squat, Power Jerk Combination
`P. clean + front squat + PJ 60% (4x1-2)`
→ 1 power clean, 1 front squat, followed by 4 power jerks — for 2 sets.

### Example Six: Light Jog
→ Jog for up to 15 minutes at a leisurely pace. The athlete should not feel any significant muscle fatigue, especially in the legs. Important for recovery that these guidelines are followed.

### Example Seven: Technique Work
`Snatch w bar` / `clean and jerk w bar`
→ "Snatch with empty bar" and "clean and jerk with empty bar." These are purely technical work and are not counted in training volume.

## Twelve-Week Program: How to Do It

### In a Perfect World

In a perfect world, the lifter would:
- Wake up at 5:00–6:00am: stretches, technique with stick, short jog (~6 minutes)
- Return to bed, sleep until 8:30am, breakfast at ~9:00am
- **Session 1 (9:30–10:00am):** squats, pulls, and abdominal work
- Lunch and rest
- **Session 2 (4:00–5:00pm):** snatch work, 20-minute break, then C&J work and abdominal work
- Supper, optional light bodybuilding session

This requires training 6 days per week. Days shown as single sessions can be split into 2 sessions per day timed per the above.

### In America

Most American weightlifters are not full-time athletes — most have side jobs, full-time jobs, or careers. The following sample macrocycles are included for different training arrangements:

- **6 days/week** — recommended for elite and international athletes
- **5 days/week** — for advanced level athletes
- **4 days/week** — for intermediate athletes
- **3 days/week** — for recreational athletes (including beginners and most Masters)
